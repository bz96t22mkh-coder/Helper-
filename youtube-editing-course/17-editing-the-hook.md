# Module 17 — Editing the Hook

> **In short:** More viewers leave in the first few seconds than at any other point. This module teaches you to win the first 5, 15, and 30 seconds on purpose.

The start of a video is where the most viewers are lost. It's also the part that most rewards editing out of order. This module treats the hook as its own separate problem from the rest of the edit.

## Why the hook is different

A viewer who's ten minutes into a video already trusts it. They're judging individual moments now. A viewer in the **first few seconds** is judging the whole idea: is this worth my time at all? The hook's only job is to answer that question fast. Everything from Modules 7–16 still applies here, but the hook has zero room for a slow start.

## The first 5 seconds

This is the highest-leverage few seconds in the whole video. The goal: show, right away, **what's happening and why it's interesting**. Not a full explanation — just enough that a scrolling viewer's thumb stops.

- Open on the most eye-catching or emotional moment you have — even if it happened later in the footage (more on this below), not just the start of the recording.
- Avoid a slow, chronological wind-up ("hey guys, so today we're going to...") as the very first thing shown — even if the creator's real intro is great personality content, it usually belongs **after** the hook, not before it.
- A strong hook often pairs a striking visual with a punchy line, or on-screen text that frames what's happening in one glance.

## The first 15 seconds

Now that you have attention, this window needs to make clear **what kind of video this is** — a funny reaction, an intense gameplay run, a specific challenge. This is where a short version of the creator's real intro can go, tightened hard (Module 16) — no dead air, no slow build.

## The first 30 seconds

By now, the viewer should have a clear sense of what's being attempted, reacted to, or explored — and a taste of the video's energy, through at least one real joke, reaction, or moment of the promised content — enough that continuing to watch feels like an easy, obvious choice.

## Using clips from later in the video

This is a standard, honest technique — not misleading, as long as you're not making something up. Take a genuinely great moment from later in the footage (a big reaction, a funny fail, a dramatic turn) and place it in the first few seconds, sometimes with a quick "...and then this happened" tease, cutting back to the real start right after.

**When to use it:** whenever the strongest, most exciting moment in the footage doesn't happen to be near the start — which, in raw gaming or reaction footage, is most of the time.

**How to use it honestly:** the teased moment should actually appear in the video later, shown truthfully — not exaggerated or shown out of context to imply something that didn't happen. The goal is reordering emphasis, not tricking the viewer. A misleading hook can win one click, but it damages trust and hurts the channel long-term.

## How to build a hook, step by step

1. After your Module 13/14 tagging pass, find the single strongest 3–8 second moment in the whole raw footage.
2. Build a hook sequence: that moment (or a quick tease of it) → a fast beat that shows what the video is actually about → straight into the real chronological start.
3. Watch just this first 30 seconds by itself, as if you're seeing this channel for the first time. If it doesn't clearly answer "what is this, and why should I keep watching?" by the end of that window, it needs another pass.
