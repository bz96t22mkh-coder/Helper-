# Module 11 — Sound Design

Sound is processed faster than sight — an unsynced or poorly balanced sound effect is one of the fastest ways to make an otherwise good edit feel amateurish, and conversely, well-executed sound design can make a mediocre visual moment feel genuinely satisfying. This module covers the SFX layer specifically (music is Module 12).

## The core SFX categories and what they're for

- **Impacts**: a hit/thud sound synced to a visual jolt (a punch-in landing, a freeze frame appearing, a hard cut). Gives visual movement physical weight.
- **Whooshes**: a swelling/sweeping sound synced to fast motion (a quick zoom, a fast cut transition, a meme sliding in). Sells the sense of speed.
- **Pops**: a light, quick sound for small UI-style moments (text appearing, a small meme popping in). Keeps small moments feeling crisp rather than silent-and-flat.
- **Record scratches**: a classic "everything stops" comedic sting, usually paired with a freeze frame — signals "wait, what?" and is best used sparingly since its comedic meaning is very specific and gets old fast if overused.
- **Comedic sounds** (boings, slide whistles, cartoon effects): used for absurd/silly moments — match intensity to how genuinely silly the moment is; using a cartoonish sound on a moment that isn't actually that silly can undercut it.
- **Dramatic sounds** (risers, booms, tension stings): used for mock-serious or genuinely suspenseful moments (Module 8's "dramatic emphasis" technique).
- **Silence**: the absence of sound, used deliberately — covered below.

## Layering sounds

A single well-chosen SFX often does more work than several stacked together. When you do layer (e.g., a whoosh under a zoom *and* an impact at the landing point), make sure each layer is doing a distinct job — the whoosh sells the movement, the impact sells the landing — rather than two sounds doing the same job and just creating clutter. If you can't articulate what each layered sound is individually contributing, cut one.

## Volume control

- Sound effects should generally sit **louder and more forward than music**, but never so loud they clip (distort) or compete with the creator's voice, which stays the priority element at all times (Module 6's Fairlight/Inspector volume controls, same mechanic as any audio clip).
- Vary SFX volume slightly to match the size of the moment — a huge reaction earns a louder, more prominent hit; a small comedic beat gets a lighter touch. Using the same volume for every SFX regardless of the moment's size is a common flattening mistake.
- Check your mix on both **headphones and a phone speaker** before finalizing — phone speakers have much less range, and a sound that felt balanced on studio headphones can disappear or turn to noise on a phone.

## Audio transitions

- A hard SFX cut is usually fine and often preferred (matches the fast pattern-interrupt pacing of this style), but a very short (2–4 frame) fade-out can smooth an SFX that would otherwise end abruptly mid-tone.
- When swapping from one ambient/background sound bed to another (e.g., game audio ducking under a big sting), a quick fade rather than an instant cut usually feels less jarring — reserve instant, hard swaps for when the *jarring* quality itself is the intended effect.

## Making sound effects satisfying rather than annoying

This is the actual skill this module is building toward. The difference:

- **Satisfying**: synced precisely to the visual moment (frame-accurate, not "close enough"), volume-matched to the moment's size, varied across the video rather than the same three sounds on repeat, and used only where a moment genuinely benefits from an audio hit.
- **Annoying**: slightly off-sync (even a couple of frames reads as "off" to viewers, even if they can't say why), used on every single cut regardless of size, the same handful of sounds repeated until they become white noise, or stacked so densely that the ear never gets a break.

**Practical fix for "annoying" sound design**: watch your rough cut back with your eyes closed, listening only. If the audio alone feels exhausting or repetitive without the visuals to distract from it, it needs thinning out — remove the least essential SFX first, and vary your remaining choices across the categories in your Module 5 library rather than reaching for the same go-to sound every time.

## Well-timed silence as a sound-design choice

Pulling *all* sound (music, SFX, even ducking game audio low) for a beat right before or after a loud sequence is itself a technique — the sudden quiet is a pattern interrupt exactly like a loud sound would be, and it's what makes the *return* of sound feel like a release. Reserve it for moments that deserve the weight — using it constantly defeats the purpose the same way overusing any other technique does.
