# Module 11 — Sound Design

> **In short:** Sound gets processed by the brain faster than what you see. Get it slightly out of sync or overused, and the whole edit feels amateur. Get it right, and even a small moment feels satisfying.

This module covers sound effects specifically. Music is covered in Module 12.

## The main types of sound effects

- **Impacts:** a hit or thud synced to a visual jolt (a punch-in landing, a hard cut). Gives movement real weight.
- **Whooshes:** a sweeping sound synced to fast motion (a quick zoom, a fast transition). Sells the feeling of speed.
- **Pops:** a light, quick sound for small moments (text appearing, a small meme popping in). Keeps small moments feeling sharp instead of flat.
- **Record scratches:** a classic "everything stops" sound, usually paired with a freeze frame. Says "wait, what?" Use it sparingly — its meaning is very specific, and it gets old fast if overused.
- **Comedic sounds** (boings, slide whistles, cartoon sounds): for silly, absurd moments. Match the sound's silliness to how silly the moment actually is.
- **Dramatic sounds** (risers, booms, tension stings): for mock-serious or genuinely tense moments (Module 8's "dramatic emphasis").
- **Silence:** the absence of sound, used on purpose — covered below.

## Layering sounds

One well-chosen sound often does more work than several stacked together. If you do layer sounds (say, a whoosh under a zoom *and* an impact when it lands), make sure each one is doing a different job — the whoosh sells the movement, the impact sells the landing. If you can't say what each layer is adding, cut one.

## Volume

- Sound effects usually sit **louder than music**, but never so loud they distort, and never louder than the creator's voice, which always stays the priority.
- Change the volume slightly to match the size of the moment. A huge reaction earns a louder hit. A small comedic beat gets a lighter touch. Using the same volume for everything flattens the whole video.
- Check your mix on **headphones and a phone speaker** before finishing. Phone speakers have much less range — a mix that sounds balanced on headphones can disappear or turn to noise on a phone.

## Sound transitions

- A hard cut between sounds is usually fine, and often preferred (it matches this style's fast pacing). A short 2–4 frame fade-out can smooth a sound that would otherwise end abruptly.
- When switching from one background sound to another (say, ducking game audio under a big sting), a quick fade usually feels smoother than a hard swap — unless the jarring feeling *is* the point.

## How to make sound satisfying, not annoying

This is the real skill this module builds.

**Satisfying sound design:**
- Synced exactly to the visual moment — not just "close enough."
- Volume matched to how big the moment is.
- Varied across the video, not the same three sounds on repeat.
- Only used where a moment actually benefits from it.

**Annoying sound design:**
- Slightly off from the visual — even a couple of frames off feels wrong, even if the viewer can't say why.
- Used on every single cut, no matter how small.
- The same handful of sounds, over and over, until they become background noise.
- So dense the ear never gets a break.

**A quick fix:** watch your rough cut with your eyes closed, listening only. If the audio alone feels tiring or repetitive without the visuals to distract you, it needs thinning out. Cut the least important sounds first, and use more of your Module 5 library's variety instead of reaching for the same go-to sound every time.

## Silence as a tool

Pulling all sound out — music, sound effects, even ducking game audio low — for a beat right before or after something loud is its own technique. The sudden quiet grabs attention just like a loud sound would. It's also what makes the *return* of sound feel like a release. Save it for moments that deserve the weight. Using it all the time defeats the purpose, the same as overusing any other technique.
