# Module 13 — Gaming Video Editing

> **In short:** Raw gameplay recording is not a video — it's raw material. This module teaches you how to turn hours of footage into one tight story.

## What to look for on your first watch-through

Before cutting anything, watch the whole raw footage once, straight through. Take timestamp notes (Module 20 shows exactly when this happens in the full process). Tag each moment as one of these:

- **Funny moments** — jokes, banter, funny accidents.
- **Important gameplay** — real skill moments, key decisions, anything the story needs to make sense.
- **Reactions** — the creator's face or voice reacting strongly, even if the in-game event is small.
- **Jokes** — planned or spontaneous bits, running gags.
- **Story moments** — anything that builds a thread (a goal being set, a twist, a callback to something earlier).
- **Suspense** — build-up toward an uncertain outcome (low health, a risky attempt, a close call).
- **Boring sections** — necessary for the player, but not interesting to watch (walking to an objective, menus, waiting).
- **Repetitive sections** — the same kind of moment happening again with nothing new added (the fifth near-identical death in a row).
- **Unexpected moments** — anything that surprises even you, the editor, on the first watch. If it surprised you, it'll probably surprise the viewer too.

## Turning raw gameplay into a story

1. **Find the spine.** What's the real throughline of this session — a goal, a challenge, a boss fight? Everything else supports that spine. Nothing else gets equal weight.
2. **Cut or shorten the boring and repeated parts, hard.** A viewer doesn't need to see every failed attempt at something. Show the funniest or most notable attempt, then skip or compress straight to the one that changes the outcome (Module 16 covers the exact tools for this).
3. **Keep the moments that build tension**, even the quieter ones. A suspenseful low-health moment matters to the story, even without a joke attached.
4. **Let it build.** If the raw footage naturally goes from casual to chaotic, keep that shape. If the funniest moment happened early and then things got repetitive, consider reordering — as long as you're not showing something that didn't really happen. This is a legitimate editing choice, not deception.
5. **Give the ending room.** Whatever the story was building toward — the boss beaten, the run ending hilariously — give that moment real space (Module 7's "let it breathe" rule) instead of rushing into the next scene.

## Real judgment calls you'll face

- **A boring but necessary stretch** (like the player traveling somewhere): speed it up (Module 6), or use a quick series of cuts, instead of showing it full-length or cutting it out and confusing the viewer about how they got from A to B.
- **A repeated failure that's actually funny *because* it repeats:** this is an exception to "cut repetition." Sometimes the pattern itself is the joke — three fails in a row, each more absurd than the last. Ask yourself: is the repetition building to something, or just stalling?
- **A big skill moment with no reaction attached:** still worth keeping if it matters to the story, but it might need extra help — a zoom (Module 9), a sound effect (Module 11) — since it won't get a boost from the creator's own reaction the way an emotional moment would.
