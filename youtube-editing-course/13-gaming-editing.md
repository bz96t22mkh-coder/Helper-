# Module 13 — Gaming Video Editing

Raw gameplay recording is not a video — it's material. This module is about the specific judgment call at the heart of gaming editing: turning several hours (or several attempts) of raw footage into a tight story with a clear shape.

## What to look for on your first watch-through

Before cutting anything, watch the full raw footage once, straight through, taking timestamp notes (Module 20 covers exactly when this happens in the full workflow). Tag each moment you notice as one of:

- **Funny moments** — jokes, banter, funny in-game accidents.
- **Important gameplay** — genuine skill moments, key decisions, anything the story needs to make sense (a boss fight, a big choice, the actual objective).
- **Reactions** — the creator's face/voice reacting strongly to something, even if the in-game event itself is minor.
- **Jokes** — planned or spontaneous bits, running gags.
- **Story moments** — anything that builds a narrative thread (a goal being set, a twist, a callback to something earlier).
- **Suspense** — build-up toward an uncertain outcome (low health, a risky attempt, a close call).
- **Boring sections** — necessary-for-the-player but uninteresting-for-a-viewer stretches (walking to an objective, menu navigation, waiting).
- **Repetitive sections** — the same type of moment happening again with nothing new added (the fifth near-identical death in a row).
- **Unexpected moments** — anything that surprises even you on the first watch; if it surprised the editor, it will very likely surprise the viewer too.

## Turning raw gameplay into a story

1. **Identify the spine.** What's the actual throughline of this session — a goal being pursued, a challenge being attempted, a boss being fought? Everything else is edited *in service of* that spine, not as equally-weighted content.
2. **Cut or compress the boring and repetitive sections aggressively.** A viewer doesn't need to see every attempt at something that failed the same way three times — show the funniest/most notable attempt, then compress or skip straight to the one that changes the outcome (Module 16 covers pacing mechanics for this).
3. **Preserve the moments that build the spine's tension**, even if they're individually "quieter" — a suspenseful low-health moment matters to the story even without a joke attached to it.
4. **Sequence for escalation where the raw footage allows it.** If the raw recording naturally builds from casual to chaotic, honor that shape. If the funniest moment happened early and then things got repetitive, consider whether reordering (without misleading the viewer about what actually happened) makes the edited story land better — this is a legitimate editing choice, not deception, as long as you're not fabricating outcomes that didn't happen.
5. **Land the payoff.** Whatever the spine was building toward (the boss defeated, the objective failed hilariously, the run ending) — make sure the edit gives that moment real space (Module 7's "let it breathe" principle) rather than rushing past it into the next scene.

## Practical judgment calls

- **A boring stretch that's still functionally necessary** (e.g., the player has to travel somewhere): compress via a speed ramp (Module 6) or a quick montage of cuts, rather than either leaving it full-length or cutting it entirely and confusing the viewer about how they got from A to B.
- **A repetitive failure sequence that's actually funny *because* it repeats**: this is an exception to "cut repetition" — sometimes the joke is the pattern itself (three fails in a row escalating in absurdity). Use judgment: is the repetition building something, or just stalling?
- **A big skill moment with no reaction attached**: still worth keeping if it matters to the story, but consider whether it needs added emphasis (Module 9's zoom, Module 11's SFX) since it won't get help from the creator's own reaction the way a big emotional moment would.
