# Module 22 — Practical Training

Theory doesn't build editing instinct — reps do. Work through these in order; each assumes the skills from the previous ones. Use real (or realistic practice) gaming/reaction footage where possible. For every exercise, self-score against the feedback criteria before moving on — better yet, have the creator or another editor look at it too.

## Exercise 1 — Edit 60 seconds of raw gaming footage

**Task:** Take 3–5 minutes of raw, unedited gaming footage and cut it down to a tight 60 seconds that tells a small, complete story (a beginning, an attempt, an outcome).

**Feedback criteria:**
- Does the 60 seconds feel complete, not like an arbitrary chunk cut off mid-thought?
- Is there any dead time left in (Module 16)?
- Are the cuts clean (no stray frames, no jump-cut jitter you didn't intend)?

## Exercise 2 — Practice clean cuts

**Task:** Take one continuous clip and practice 10 different cut points using the Blade tool and ripple delete (Module 6), each removing a different small section, without leaving a gap or a stray audio pop at the cut point.

**Feedback criteria:**
- Is every cut point invisible/seamless unless it's meant to be a hard, noticeable cut?
- Is there any audio glitch (a pop, a cut-off word) at any cut point?
- Did ripple delete correctly close every gap?

## Exercise 3 — Practice comedic zooms

**Task:** Take five different moments of varying intensity (per Module 9's exercise 2) and apply a matched zoom treatment to each — subtle punch-in, hard cut-zoom, and a slower dramatic zoom, at least one of each across the five.

**Feedback criteria:**
- Does each zoom's intensity match its moment's intensity (Module 8's pairing table)?
- Is the timing locked to the beat of the moment, not early or late?
- Does any zoom show visible quality degradation from over-scaling (Module 9)?

## Exercise 4 — Practice sound effects

**Task:** Take one minute of footage with at least five distinct moments, and add a matched sound effect from your Module 5 library to each, varying the category (don't use the same sound twice).

**Feedback criteria:**
- Is each SFX synced frame-accurately to its visual moment?
- Is the volume of each SFX matched to the size of its moment, not uniform?
- Does the sequence, listened to with eyes closed (Module 11's test), feel satisfying rather than cluttered?

## Exercise 5 — Practice meme timing

**Task:** Take one minute of footage and identify 2–3 genuinely meme-worthy moments (not more — Module 10's restraint principle), place an appropriate meme from your library on each, with correct timing, duration, and placement.

**Feedback criteria:**
- Does each meme match the specific emotional category of its moment?
- Is placement/size correct (not blocking key content, legible at small scale)?
- Would each moment still be reasonably funny without the meme (Module 10's enhancing-vs-forcing test) — i.e., is the meme adding, not compensating for a flat moment?

## Exercise 6 — Edit a funny reaction

**Task:** Take 2–3 minutes of raw reaction footage (the creator reacting to something) and edit it into a tight, engaging piece, applying Module 14's principles — emphasizing the reaction, cutting/compressing dead "just watching" stretches.

**Feedback criteria:**
- Is there any stretch where the viewer is just watching the source material with no added value (Module 14's core test)?
- Are the strongest facial/verbal reactions given appropriate emphasis (zoom, timing, or just clean space to land)?
- Does the pacing avoid both boredom and exhaustion (Module 16)?

## Exercise 7 — Turn boring footage into an engaging sequence

**Task:** Deliberately pick the least eventful, most repetitive stretch of raw footage you have (menu navigation, walking, a repeated failed attempt) and edit it into something watchable using compression, speed ramps, pattern interrupts, and/or reordering — without fabricating anything that didn't happen.

**Feedback criteria:**
- Does the result feel meaningfully more watchable than the raw footage, honestly assessed?
- Which specific technique(s) from Module 16 did the fix rely on, and was that the right tool for this specific kind of boring (too slow vs. too repetitive)?
- Does it still communicate accurately what actually happened (no misleading implication)?

## Exercise 8 — Create a complete 3–5 minute gaming edit

**Task:** Take a full gaming footage session and produce a complete, polished 3–5 minute video: full workflow (Module 20), including a proper hook (Module 17), comedic layer (Modules 8–12), and a full pass against the Module 21 Quality Checklist.

**Feedback criteria:** Run the full Module 21 checklist. Additionally:
- Does the video have a clear spine/story (Module 13)?
- Does the first 30 seconds pass the "cold viewer" test (Module 17)?
- Would this be ready to actually send to a creator for feedback, or does it still read as a practice exercise?

## Exercise 9 — Create a complete reaction edit

**Task:** Same as Exercise 8, but for a full reaction video, applying Module 14's specific principles throughout.

**Feedback criteria:** Same Module 21 checklist pass, plus Module 14's "value coming from the creator, not the source material" test applied to the whole video, not just one stretch.

## Exercise 10 — Edit a complete YouTube video from start to finish

**Task:** Using real (or the most realistic available) footage and an Editor Brief (Module 19, filled out by the creator or simulated as realistically as possible), run the entire Module 20 workflow end to end — including a feedback round using the actual templates, revisions, and a Final Approval Checklist pass.

**Feedback criteria:** This is the capstone — score it against everything in Modules 7, 8, 16, and 21 simultaneously, plus:
- Did the communication system (Module 19) actually get used as intended, not skipped under time pressure?
- Looking at the whole process, where did the most time get spent, and does that match where Module 20 says priority should be (structure/pacing before polish)?
- Is this a video you'd be comfortable being credited as the editor on?
