# Section 9 — Critical Thinking

**Est. length:** 8–12 sessions (core track: 9 days).
**Prerequisite:** Section 1 (Meta-Learning). **Feeds into:** Debating (direct prerequisite), Problem-Solving, Decision-Making, Strategic Thinking.

---

## 1. What You Need to Learn
Logic, argument structure, evidence, assumptions, cognitive biases, logical fallacies, counterarguments, steelmanning, evidence evaluation, and probabilistic thinking.

## 2. Why It Matters
This is the reasoning engine underneath Debating (next), Problem-Solving, Decision-Making, and Strategic Thinking. The goal is to reason *accurately*, not to win — this section trains you to spot bad arguments (including your own) before you build a debate style on top of them.

## 3. Foundational Concepts
- **An argument has a structure:** a claim, supported by reasons, backed by evidence — evaluating any argument means checking each link, not just whether the conclusion feels right.
- **Every argument rests on assumptions:** most disagreements are actually disagreements about an unstated assumption, not the visible claim.
- **Your brain takes shortcuts (biases) by default:** these shortcuts are usually useful but systematically distort reasoning in predictable, nameable ways — the fix is knowing the patterns, not "trying harder to be objective."

## 4. Beginner Concepts
- **Claim / Reason / Evidence:** breaking any argument (yours or someone else's) into these three parts before evaluating it.
- **Surfacing assumptions:** asking "what would have to be true for this claim to hold?" to find the hidden premise.
- **Common cognitive biases:** confirmation bias (favoring info that confirms what you believe), availability bias (overweighting vivid/recent examples), sunk-cost fallacy (justifying more investment because of past investment).

## 5. Intermediate Concepts
- **Common logical fallacies:** straw man (attacking a distorted version of the argument), ad hominem (attacking the person, not the argument), false dichotomy (only two options presented when more exist), slippery slope (assuming one step inevitably leads to an extreme outcome), appeal to authority (treating a claim as true just because an authority said it).
- **Counterarguments:** actively generating the strongest objection to your own position before someone else raises it.
- **Steelmanning:** stating the opposing view in its strongest, most charitable form — often stronger than how its actual proponents state it — before responding to it.

## 6. Advanced Concepts
- **Evidence evaluation:** source quality, sample size, correlation vs. causation, and recognizing when evidence is anecdotal vs. representative.
- **Probabilistic thinking:** reasoning in likelihoods ("probably," "60% chance," "unlikely but possible") instead of binary true/false, and updating your confidence as new evidence arrives.
- **Applying this to your own beliefs:** using the same rigor on positions you already hold as on ones you're evaluating fresh — the hardest and most valuable application.

## 7. Practical Exercises
- **Claim-Reason-Evidence breakdown:** take a real argument (an article, a claim you heard, an opinion of yours) and break it into its 3 parts.
- **Assumption-hunt:** for 3 opinions you hold, write the unstated assumption each depends on.
- **Bias-spotting log:** notice and name one instance of your own cognitive bias in action each day.
- **Steelman drill:** pick a position you disagree with and write its strongest possible version before responding to it.

## 8. Drills
- **Fallacy-spotting drill:** read/watch a debate, opinion piece, or ad, and identify any fallacies used.
- **Counterargument-first drill:** before defending your own opinion on something, write the single strongest objection to it.
- **Confidence-calibration drill:** state a factual belief with a probability attached ("I'm 70% sure that...") and check it against a source afterward.

## 9. Real-World Applications
- Use claim-reason-evidence breakdown on the next opinion piece or argument you encounter (news, social media, a conversation).
- Steelman the other side the next time you're in a real disagreement, before responding.
- Apply probabilistic language ("I think it's likely, not certain, that...") in your next uncertain claim, including in trading-related reasoning.

## 10. Challenges
- Take a strongly-held belief of yours and genuinely steelman the best opposing case — see if your confidence shifts at all.
- Find 3 fallacies "in the wild" this week (ads, social media, conversation) and write what the fallacy actually is and what a valid version of the argument would look like.

## 11. Assessments

**Baseline (Day 1):** Take a real opinion piece, ad, or argument you've recently seen. Break it into claim/reason/evidence and identify any weak links, without any instruction yet — just your current instinct. Send me your breakdown.

**Exit (Day 9):** Take a real, current debate topic (yours or a real one). Steelman both sides, identify at least one fallacy or weak assumption on each side, and state your own position with a probability/confidence level attached, explaining what would change your mind.

## 12. Mastery Criteria

| Level | Looks like |
|---|---|
| Beginner | Evaluates arguments by gut feeling; doesn't notice fallacies or hidden assumptions |
| Developing | Can break down an argument with prompting; struggles to steelman views they disagree with |
| Competent | Reliably identifies claim/reason/evidence, common fallacies, and states confidence levels |
| Advanced | Steelmans opposing views fairly; catches biases in real time, including their own |
| Highly Proficient | Applies rigorous reasoning fluidly in live conversation, not just written analysis |
| Mastery | Default mode of thinking is calibrated, evidence-based, and self-critical |

Competent+ needed to progress to Section 10.
