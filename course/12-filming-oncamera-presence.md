# Module 12 — Filming & On-Camera Presence

## 1. Concept

On-camera presence is the combination of your physical setup (what makes you watchable/listenable at a technical level) and your performance habits (what makes you watchable at a human level). Both are trainable; neither requires expensive gear or natural charisma to start.

## 2. Why it matters

Poor audio or lighting causes viewers to leave regardless of how good your content is — technical quality is a floor, not a differentiator, but falling below the floor caps everything else in this course. Weak body language/energy undercuts even a well-scripted, well-lit video. Get both to "good enough," then let personality (Module 2) and talking skill (Module 10) do the real work.

## 3. Technical setup priorities (in order of impact)

1. **Audio** — the single most important technical factor. Viewers tolerate mediocre video far more than bad audio. A basic USB or lav microphone dramatically outperforms a laptop/webcam mic. Record in a room with soft surfaces (curtains, carpet, furniture) to reduce echo — this matters more than the mic price tag.
2. **Lighting** — even, soft light on your face (a window facing you, or a basic ring/softbox light) beats a harsh single overhead light or backlighting that silhouettes you. Avoid being lit from behind by a bright window.
3. **Framing** — eye-level or slightly above, not looking down at you. Leave appropriate headroom (not too much empty space above your head). Chest-up or waist-up framing is standard for reaction/commentary; for gaming, decide if you want a facecam and how large — bigger facecam emphasizes personality, smaller emphasizes gameplay.
4. **Background** — should be simple enough not to distract but personal enough to add character/brand identity (posters, LED accents, game-relevant items) — avoid a completely blank wall (feels generic) or a cluttered one (distracting).
5. **Camera quality** — matters least of these five. A decent phone or webcam is enough to start; upgrade only once audio, lighting, and framing are solid and you have a reason (revenue, comfort) to invest further.

## 4. Performance habits

- **Eye contact**: look into the lens, not at your own preview screen — this is what makes viewers feel spoken to directly rather than watched from the side.
- **Facial expressions**: slightly exaggerate real reactions for camera — expressions that feel natural in person often read as flat on camera; push a bit further than feels necessary.
- **Body language**: open, animated gestures read as confident and engaging; hunched/still posture reads as low energy even if your voice isn't. Lean in during tense/exciting moments; this alone communicates stakes.
- **Voice**: vary pace and pitch — monotone delivery is one of the fastest ways to lose viewers regardless of content quality. Slow down for emphasis, speed up for excitement/chaos.
- **Confidence signals**: don't apologize for mistakes on camera (a stumbled line, a game death) — acknowledge and move forward, or turn it into a bit. Over-apologizing reads as low confidence and slows pacing.
- **Natural reactions**: don't suppress genuine reactions to look composed — genuine surprise/frustration/excitement is core content, not a flaw to hide (Module 10).

## 5. Preparing before filming

- Know your hook and CTA precisely (scripted per Module 9); know your setup points as bullets; leave the body open.
- Do a 2–3 minute vocal warm-up (just talk out loud about anything) before hitting record — cold-starting into a hook often sounds flat.
- Check audio levels and framing with a 10-second test clip before a full session — catching a bad mic angle after an hour of recording is a lost session.
- Have water nearby; know your planned beats (Module 4/5) so you're not deciding structure while also performing.

## 6. Filming efficiently

- Batch similar content when possible (record multiple reaction segments or multiple gaming sessions in one sitting if energy allows) — reduces per-video setup/teardown overhead.
- Film pickup lines/inserts (hook re-takes, missed CTA, a clarifying line) immediately after the main session while context is fresh, rather than scheduling a separate session later.
- Note your own timestamp markers verbally during filming ("mark that, that was a good moment") — this becomes free guidance for your editor (Module 13) instead of them having to comb raw footage blind.

## 7. Examples

- A creator angles a desk lamp with a diffuser toward their face instead of buying an expensive light kit — solves 90% of the lighting problem at low cost.
- A creator does a 3-minute out-loud rant about their day before every recording session specifically to warm up vocal energy before the hook is filmed.

## 8. Exercises

1. Record a 60-second test clip in your actual filming setup. Check audio clarity, lighting evenness, framing, and background against Section 3.
2. Record a 2-minute unscripted talk direct-to-camera. Review for eye contact (lens vs. screen), vocal variation, and body language/energy.
3. Do a full pre-filming warm-up (Section 5) before your next real session and note whether your opening hook felt noticeably better than cold-starting.

## 9. Templates

**Pre-filming checklist card:**
```
[ ] Audio test clip reviewed (no clipping, no heavy echo)
[ ] Lighting even on face, no harsh backlight
[ ] Framing at eye level, correct headroom
[ ] Background checked (not blank, not cluttered)
[ ] Hook and CTA lines rehearsed
[ ] Vocal warm-up done
[ ] Beats/setup bullets reviewed (not the full script)
```

## 10. Checklist

- [ ] Audio is clear with minimal room echo/background noise
- [ ] Face is evenly lit, not silhouetted or harshly shadowed
- [ ] Eye contact is with the lens, not the preview screen
- [ ] Voice has pace/pitch variation, not monotone
- [ ] Verbal timestamp markers used for standout moments

## 11. Common mistakes

- Prioritizing an expensive camera before fixing audio and lighting, which matter more.
- Sitting still and flat-toned during high-stakes moments, undercutting the content's actual energy.
- Skipping the vocal warm-up and delivering a flat, cold hook that then needs a costly re-shoot.

## 12. What beginners waste time on

- Gear-shopping (cameras, elaborate lighting rigs) before solving the cheap, high-impact basics (audio, simple lighting, framing).
- Re-recording entire sessions over a single flubbed line instead of doing a quick pickup insert and flagging it for the editor.

## 13. Homework

Run the Pre-Filming Checklist Card before your next recording session and film at least 5 minutes of content start to finish using it.

## 14. Checkpoint

Watch back your 5-minute session. Audio should be clear, lighting even, eye contact on the lens most of the time, and vocal energy varied. If any of these fail, isolate and fix that one variable before your next full recording session.
