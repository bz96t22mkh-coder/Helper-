# Module 20 — Brand Deals

## 1. Concept

A brand deal (sponsorship) is a paid partnership where a brand pays for exposure to your specific audience, usually via a dedicated segment, full video, or social post. Brands aren't paying for your subscriber count alone — they're paying for the trust and attention your specific audience gives you, which is why brand fit and authentic integration matter more than raw size.

## 2. How brands choose creators

Brands (or the agencies representing them) typically evaluate: audience size and relevant demographic fit (Module 17's audience demographics data), engagement rate (comments/likes relative to views — a strong signal of real influence over passive reach), content quality and consistency, brand safety (nothing that would embarrass the sponsor), and genuine niche relevance (a gaming-peripheral brand wants a gaming audience specifically, not just any large audience).

## 3. Media kit

A one-page (or short deck) document you present to brands, summarizing your channel as a business opportunity, not just a fan page.

**Media kit template:**
```
CHANNEL NAME + one-line brand description (per Module 2)
Subscriber count + monthly views/impressions
Audience demographics (age, geography, gender split — from Module 17)
Engagement rate (average likes+comments / views)
Content pillars (per Module 3)
Cross-platform reach (Module 15 — TikTok/Instagram/etc. follower counts if relevant)
Past brand partnerships (if any) with results/testimonials
Available sponsorship formats (dedicated segment, full video, social post, bundle)
Rate card or "rates available on request"
Contact info
```

## 4. Pricing

There's no single universal formula — rates vary by niche, engagement rate, and format — but a common starting reference point many creators use is a rough per-1,000-views (CPM-style) baseline for a dedicated segment, adjusted up for higher engagement rates, exclusivity requests, or usage rights, and adjusted for the specific deliverable (a 60-second segment vs. a full dedicated video vs. a bundle across YouTube + social are different rates). Research current going rates in your specific niche/size bracket before quoting — rates shift over time and by category.

## 5. Negotiation framework

- Know your minimum acceptable rate before entering a conversation, based on your researched market rate and your own time/effort cost, not the brand's opening offer.
- Negotiate on more than just price: usage rights (how long/where the brand can reuse your content), exclusivity (are you barred from competing brands, for how long), deliverable count (one video vs. a bundle), and payment timeline.
- Don't undervalue yourself to "get the first deal" if it also means underpricing every future negotiation — some creators accept a below-market first deal deliberately for portfolio/testimonial value; if you do this, be intentional and time-limited about it, not a permanent pattern.
- Be willing to walk away from a genuinely bad-fit brand or lowball offer — a bad sponsorship can cost more in audience trust (Module 2) than it earns in revenue.

## 6. Outreach (when you're pitching brands, not being approached)

Keep it short, specific, and tailored — a generic mass-blasted pitch email underperforms a short, personalized one referencing why this specific brand fits your specific audience. Include: who you are (one line), your audience fit for their product specifically, 1–2 relevant stats (from your media kit), and a clear, low-friction next step (a call, or straight to your media kit/rate card).

## 7. Contracts and deliverables

Get terms in writing even for informal-feeling deals — deliverables (exact format, length, posting date), payment terms and timeline, usage rights, exclusivity clauses (if any), and required disclosures (see Section 9). Clarify revision rounds allowed on sponsored content before starting, same principle as Module 13's editor communication.

## 8. Usage rights and exclusivity — what to watch for

- **Usage rights**: how long, and on what platforms, can the brand reuse your content (e.g., in their own ads)? Broader/longer usage rights are worth more and should be priced accordingly — don't grant unlimited perpetual usage rights at the same price as a single-post rate.
- **Exclusivity**: does the deal bar you from working with competing brands, for how long? Exclusivity should command a premium since it closes off other revenue during that window.

## 9. Legal/disclosure requirement

Sponsored content must be clearly disclosed to your audience (per your jurisdiction's advertising regulations and YouTube's own paid-promotion disclosure tools) — this isn't optional and isn't just a platform checkbox; audience trust (Module 2) depends on transparency about what's paid content.

## 10. Red flags

- Payment only after posting with no clear contract or upfront deposit, especially with a brand you have no track record with.
- Vague or unlimited usage rights requested without corresponding pay.
- Pressure to make exaggerated or false claims about a product.
- A brand that's a poor genuine fit for your audience — even at a good rate, this risks audience trust more than it's worth.
- No clear deliverable scope, inviting scope creep (endless revisions, added "just one more thing" asks).

## 11. Examples

- A mid-sized gaming channel negotiates a dedicated-segment rate up from a brand's initial lowball offer by presenting engagement-rate data (from their media kit) showing above-average audience interaction relative to channel size.
- A creator declines a well-paying deal from a brand irrelevant to their gaming/reaction audience, correctly judging the trust cost (Module 2) would outweigh the short-term revenue.

## 12. Exercises

1. Draft your own Media Kit using the Section 3 template with your current real numbers (even if modest).
2. Research 3 comparable creators' likely sponsorship rate range in your niche/size bracket (public rate-card databases or creator community discussions are useful sources).
3. Write a short outreach email pitching a hypothetical (or real) brand genuinely relevant to your content.

## 13. Templates

**Sponsorship checklist (before agreeing to any deal):**
```
[ ] Brand is a genuine fit for my audience and won't damage trust
[ ] Deliverables clearly scoped (format, length, posting date, revision rounds)
[ ] Payment terms and timeline clear and in writing
[ ] Usage rights explicitly defined and priced accordingly
[ ] Exclusivity terms (if any) explicitly defined and priced accordingly
[ ] Disclosure requirements planned into the content
[ ] Rate meets or exceeds my researched minimum
```

## 14. Checklist

- [ ] Media kit exists and is current
- [ ] I know my minimum acceptable rate and what drives it up (usage/exclusivity)
- [ ] I have a red-flag checklist I actually apply before saying yes
- [ ] Disclosure practices are built into my process, not an afterthought

## 15. Common mistakes

- Accepting the first offer without negotiating on usage rights or exclusivity terms.
- Taking any paying brand deal regardless of audience fit, damaging long-term trust for short-term revenue.
- No written contract, leading to disputes over deliverables or payment.
- Forgetting or minimizing sponsored-content disclosure.

## 16. What beginners waste time on

- Chasing brand deals before there's a real, engaged audience and media kit to support outreach (Module 19's stage guidance applies directly here).
- Pitching brands with zero relevance to their content just because the brand pays well — low response rate and, if it lands, high trust cost.

## 17. Homework

Complete your Media Kit and Sponsorship Checklist templates, even if you're not actively pitching brands yet — have them ready for when inbound or outbound opportunities arise.

## 18. Checkpoint

You should be able to name your minimum acceptable rate range and at least 2 red flags you'd walk away from in a sponsorship offer. If you can't yet, redo the rate-research exercise before advancing.
