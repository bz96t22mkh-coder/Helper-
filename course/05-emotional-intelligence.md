# Section 5 — Emotional Intelligence

**Length:** 12 days. **Goal:** notice what you're feeling before it drives your behavior, and read other people accurately.

---

### Day 1 — Naming What You Feel

You can't manage a reaction you haven't noticed yet. Most people default to "fine," "good," or "bad" — but those are too vague to act on. Precise naming (frustrated vs. anxious vs. disappointed) tells you what's actually going on and what it's pointing at.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 15 | Baseline: describe your last strong emotional reaction — trigger, feeling, what you did, would you do it differently now? |
| 20 | Pause 3 times today. Name your current emotion as precisely as you can, and its likely trigger. |
| 15 | How hard was it to find a precise word vs. defaulting to "fine"? |
| 10 | Reflection. |

### Day 2 — The Pause

Between a trigger and your reaction there's a gap, even if it's only a second. That gap is the only place you actually get to choose your response instead of just running the old pattern.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Check-in: name your current emotion. |
| 15 | Practice a 3-count pause in 2 low-stakes moments today before responding to something mildly annoying. |
| 20 | Take yesterday's logged emotion. Write one more useful interpretation of what triggered it. |
| 20 | Did the reframe change how you'd want to respond? |

### Day 3 — Your Own Triggers

A trigger is rarely really about what just happened — it's usually pointing at a value, an insecurity, or an old pattern underneath. Knowing what's actually under your reaction lets you respond to that instead of the surface event.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Check-in. |
| 30 | List 3 situations that reliably spike a reaction in you. For each, write what's likely underneath it. |
| 15 | Pick your most frequent one — what would a regulated response look like next time? |
| 10 | Reflection. |

### Day 4 — Reflecting Feelings Before Advice

When someone shares something hard, the instinct is to jump to advice or your own story. Naming their feeling first — "that sounds frustrating" — makes them feel actually heard, which is usually what they wanted before any advice.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Check-in. |
| 30 | For 3 real or recalled moments someone shared something with you, write a feeling-reflection response first, before advice. |
| 15 | How does leading with the feeling change the likely tone of the conversation? |
| 10 | Reflection. |

### Day 5 — Checkpoint

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Check-in. |
| 30 | In one real conversation, use pause + name-the-emotion + reflect-their-feeling at least once. Report specifics. |
| 15 | Rep on weakest element. |
| 10 | Progress vs. Day 1. |

### Day 6 — Reading Others

Words and tone don't always agree — someone says "I'm fine" in a flat voice. Noticing the mismatch (without assuming what it means) is the first step; checking it directly is the second.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Check-in. |
| 25 | In a conversation today, note 2 moments where tone or body language seemed to say something different from the words. |
| 20 | What's your default when you notice a mismatch — ignore it, or check it? |
| 10 | Reflection. |

### Day 7 — What's Really Driving Someone

A stated request often isn't the real need. "I need this by Friday" might really be "I'm worried about looking bad to my boss." Digging one or two layers down changes how you respond.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Check-in. |
| 30 | Take 3 stated opinions or requests (yours or others'), dig 2–3 layers to the real need or fear underneath. |
| 15 | Does this change how you'd respond to any of them? |
| 10 | Reflection. |

### Day 8 — Setting a Boundary

A boundary stated as one clear, calm sentence — no over-explaining, no apologizing for having it — lands better than a long justification.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Check-in. |
| 20 | Identify one real boundary you need to set. Write it as a single clear sentence. |
| 15 | Practice saying it out loud until it sounds calm, not defensive. |
| 10 | What makes this hard — fear of conflict, of seeming rude, something else? |
| 10 | Reflection. |

### Day 9 — Direct and Empathic at the Same Time

You don't have to choose between keeping the peace and saying what you actually think. State your position directly, and acknowledge theirs — in the same breath, not as a trade-off.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Check-in. |
| 30 | Write out a real or plausible disagreement. Include both a direct statement of your position and an acknowledgment of theirs, in the same response. |
| 15 | Where did your first draft default to over-softening or over-hardening? |
| 10 | Reflection. |

### Day 10 — Reading a Room

One-on-one cues are one thing; a group has its own energy — who's checked out, where the tension is. Spend a minute purely observing before jumping into participating.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Check-in. |
| 25 | In your next group setting, spend the first 2 minutes purely observing before participating. Note what you noticed. |
| 20 | What did you pick up that you'd normally miss by jumping straight in? |
| 10 | Reflection. |

### Day 11 — Real-World Round

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 10 | Identify one real emotionally-loaded conversation this week — a disagreement, feedback, a boundary. |
| 35 | Have it, bringing the full toolkit. |
| 15 | Report: named emotion, empathic read, regulated response, outcome. |

### Day 12 — Exit Check

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Check-in. |
| 40 | Navigate one real emotionally-loaded conversation using a named emotion, an empathic read of the other person, and a regulated response. Report what happened. |
| 15 | Compare to Day 1's baseline — what would you do differently in that original situation now? |

**Mastery bar:** you notice and name emotion in real time, most of the time, and regulate before reacting — not just after the fact. If not yet, repeat the weakest drill (usually regulation under real pressure or boundary-setting) with new real situations.
