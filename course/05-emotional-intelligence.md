# Section 5 — Emotional Intelligence

**Length:** 12 days. **Goal:** notice what you're feeling before it drives your behavior, and read other people accurately.

---

### Day 1 — Naming What You Feel

You can't manage a reaction you haven't noticed yet. Most people default to "fine," "good," or "bad" — but those are too vague to act on. Precise naming tells you what's actually going on: frustrated (blocked from something), disappointed (expected more), anxious (uncertain about a threat) all call for different responses, but "bad" tells you nothing.

**How to do it:** a few times a day, stop and ask "what am I actually feeling right now, specifically?" Push past the first vague word — if "bad" is your first answer, ask what kind of bad. Then ask what triggered it. That's the whole drill: name it, find the trigger.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 15 | Baseline: describe your last strong emotional reaction — trigger, feeling, what you did, would you do it differently now? |
| 20 | Pause 3 times today. Name your current emotion as precisely as you can, past the first vague word, and its likely trigger. |
| 15 | How hard was it to find a precise word vs. defaulting to "fine"? |
| 10 | Reflection. |

### Day 2 — The Pause

Between a trigger and your reaction there's a gap, even if it's only a second. That gap is the only place you actually get to choose your response instead of just running the old pattern on autopilot.

**How to do it:** the instant you notice a reaction spike, count to three before you say or do anything. Not to suppress it — just to create enough space to ask "what do I actually want to do here" instead of reacting on reflex.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Check-in: name your current emotion. |
| 15 | Practice a 3-count pause in 2 low-stakes moments today before responding to something mildly annoying. |
| 20 | Take yesterday's logged emotion. Write one more useful interpretation of what triggered it. |
| 20 | Did the reframe change how you'd want to respond? |

### Day 3 — Your Own Triggers

A trigger is rarely really about what just happened — it's usually pointing at a value, an insecurity, or an old pattern underneath. Someone being 10 minutes late might trigger disproportionate anger not because of the 10 minutes, but because it taps a deeper "my time doesn't matter to people" pattern. Knowing what's actually under your reaction lets you respond to that, not just the surface event.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Check-in. |
| 30 | List 3 situations that reliably spike a reaction in you. For each, ask "what does this actually remind me of, or what does it threaten?" — write what's likely underneath it. |
| 15 | Pick your most frequent one — what would a regulated response look like next time? |
| 10 | Reflection. |

### Day 4 — Reflecting Feelings Before Advice

When someone shares something hard, the instinct is to jump to advice or your own similar story. Naming their feeling first — "that sounds frustrating" — makes them feel actually heard, which is usually what they wanted before any advice, and it takes one sentence.

**Format:** "[Feeling word] that + [what happened]." — "That sounds exhausting, dealing with that for a week straight." Say that before anything else, even if you have great advice ready.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Check-in. |
| 30 | For 3 real or recalled moments someone shared something with you, write a feeling-reflection response first, before advice. |
| 15 | How does leading with the feeling change the likely tone of the conversation? |
| 10 | Reflection. |

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Check-in. |

| 15 | Rep on weakest element. |
| 10 | Progress vs. Day 1. |

### Day 6 — Reading Others

Words and tone don't always agree — someone says "I'm fine" in a flat voice while looking away. Noticing the mismatch is step one; assuming you know what it means is a trap. Step two is checking it directly: "you said you're fine, but something seems off — am I reading that right?"

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Check-in. |
| 25 | In a conversation today, note 2 moments where tone or body language seemed to say something different from the words. |
| 20 | What's your default when you notice a mismatch — ignore it, or check it? |
| 10 | Reflection. |

### Day 7 — What's Really Driving Someone

A stated request often isn't the real need. "I need this by Friday" might really be "I'm worried about looking bad to my boss." Ask yourself: if I imagine their whole situation, not just this one request, what are they actually trying to protect or achieve? Dig one or two layers down before responding.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Check-in. |
| 30 | Take 3 stated opinions or requests (yours or others'), dig 2–3 layers to the real need or fear underneath. |
| 15 | Does this change how you'd respond to any of them? |
| 10 | Reflection. |

### Day 8 — Setting a Boundary

A boundary stated as one clear, calm sentence — no over-explaining, no apologizing for having it — lands better than a long justification, which just invites negotiation over something that isn't up for negotiation.

**Format:** "I'm not able to [thing], but I can [alternative, if there is one]." That's the whole sentence. No "sorry, I feel really bad but..." padding.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Check-in. |
| 20 | Identify one real boundary you need to set. Write it in the format above. |
| 15 | Practice saying it out loud until it sounds calm, not defensive. |
| 10 | What makes this hard — fear of conflict, of seeming rude, something else? |
| 10 | Reflection. |

### Day 9 — Direct and Empathic at the Same Time

You don't have to choose between keeping the peace and saying what you actually think. State your position directly, and acknowledge theirs — in the same breath, not as a trade-off.

**Format:** "I get why you'd see it that way, [specific reason] — I still think [your actual position], because [your reason]." Both halves, no softening your actual view into mush.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Check-in. |
| 30 | Write out a real or plausible disagreement using the format above. |
| 15 | Where did your first draft default to over-softening or over-hardening? |
| 10 | Reflection. |

### Day 10 — Reading a Room

One-on-one cues are one thing; a group has its own energy — who's checked out, where the tension is, whether the room agrees more than it's saying out loud. Spend the first minute purely observing before jumping into participating, and you'll pick up things you'd otherwise miss entirely.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Check-in. |
| 25 | In your next group setting, spend the first 2 minutes purely observing before participating. Note what you noticed. |
| 20 | What did you pick up that you'd normally miss by jumping straight in? |
| 10 | Reflection. |

### Day 11 — Real-World Round

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 10 | Identify one real emotionally-loaded conversation this week — a disagreement, feedback, a boundary. |
| 35 | Have it, bringing the full toolkit. |
| 15 | Report: named emotion, empathic read, regulated response, outcome. |

### Day 12 — Exit Check

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Check-in. |
| 40 | Navigate one real emotionally-loaded conversation using a named emotion, an empathic read of the other person, and a regulated response. Report what happened. |
| 15 | Compare to Day 1's baseline — what would you do differently in that original situation now? |

**Mastery bar:** you notice and name emotion in real time, most of the time, and regulate before reacting — not just after the fact. If not yet, repeat the weakest drill (usually regulation under real pressure or boundary-setting) with new real situations.
