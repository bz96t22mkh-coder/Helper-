# Section 3 — English & Articulation

**Length:** 24 days. **Goal:** you're a native speaker whose spoken English has gone rusty from disuse — this rebuilds fluency, not grammar from scratch.

---

## Phase A — Organizing Thought Into Speech (Days 1–5)

### Day 1 — Think, Then Speak

Jumbled speech is almost never a vocabulary problem — it's a sequencing problem. You start talking before you've decided the shape of what you're saying, so it comes out in pieces, doubles back, restarts. The fix is a deliberate 1–2 second pause before you answer, using it to pick a simple shape: **Point → Reason → Example → Point.**

**How to do it:** when a question or topic lands, don't open your mouth yet. In the pause, silently answer these four in order: (1) What's my actual point, in one sentence? (2) Why — one reason? (3) What's one concrete example? (4) Say the point again to close it. Then speak, following that order.

**Worked example.** Prompt: "What do you think about working from home?" Weak/unpaused: "Um, I guess it's like, there's pros and cons, like sometimes it's good because, I don't know, you save time but then also sometimes it's kind of isolating and, yeah." Paused + structured: "I think it's a net positive." (point) "Mainly because it gives back the commute time." (reason) "I used to lose ten hours a week just getting to and from an office — now that's ten hours back." (example) "So overall, worth it for me." (point, closed).

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 15 | Baseline: record yourself talking for 2 minutes, unscripted, about your week. Note where you stalled or lost the thread. |
| 20 | 3 random topics, 60 seconds each. Pause 2 seconds before each, silently run Point→Reason→Example→Point, then speak it. |
| 10 | Redo your weakest one, counting filler words ("um," "like"). |
| 15 | Where did you jumble — picking words, or picking the shape of the sentence? |

### Day 2 — When You Can't Find the Word

Freezing on a missing word kills momentum — the silence itself makes it worse, because now you're also panicking about the silence. Instead, describe around it and keep moving; your brain often finds the real word a few seconds later anyway, once it's not under direct pressure to produce it on command.

**How to do it:** the moment a word won't come, say what the thing *does* or *looks like* instead of stopping. "The thing you use to..." / "it's kind of like a..." / "you know, the place where..." — any of these keep the sentence moving while your brain keeps searching in the background.

**Worked example.** You're trying to say "spreadsheet" and it won't come. Instead of stopping: "I put it in — you know, that grid program, with the rows and columns, where you can do formulas — I put it in there and just tracked it that way." You never stopped talking, and the listener understood you fine even without the exact word.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 15 | Describe 10 random objects without naming them, using only what they do or look like. |
| 20 | 3 new topics, 60–90 sec each, using description instead of stalling whenever a word won't come. |
| 15 | Reflect: did describing around it keep you moving instead of freezing? |
| 10 | Warm-up: redo yesterday's weakest topic. |

### Day 3 — One Idea Per Sentence

Rambling comes from stacking two or three ideas into one sentence, connected with "and" and "so" and "which," until the listener loses the thread. Native-sounding fluency is short, complete sentences chained together — not long, complex ones.

**How to do it:** if you notice a sentence has run past about 15 words and you're still going, stop, put a period in wherever you are, and start a new sentence for the next idea. You can always connect ideas across two short sentences ("That happened. It changed everything after.") instead of jamming them into one long one.

**Worked example.** Run-on: "So I went to the meeting and it was supposed to be quick but then my manager brought up the budget thing which nobody was prepared for so we ended up going way over time and I had to reschedule my next call." Split: "I went to the meeting expecting it to be quick. My manager brought up the budget — nobody was prepared for that. It ran way over. I had to reschedule my next call." Same information, four clean beats instead of one long tangle.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up topic. |
| 20 | Take 5 run-on sentences you'd normally produce and rewrite each as 2–3 short, complete ones. |
| 20 | 2 topics, deliberately using short sentences only — stop and restart the moment you feel a sentence running long. |
| 15 | Notice: is short-sentence delivery slower or faster than your rambling default? |

### Day 4 — Harder Topics

Abstract topics are harder because there's no concrete example sitting right there waiting to be described — you have to build one. The fix is the same Point→Reason→Example→Point shape from Day 1, except now you deliberately invent a small concrete scenario for the "example" step instead of reporting a real memory.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up. |
| 30 | 4 tougher, more abstract topics ("what makes a good decision," "why people procrastinate"), 90 sec each — for the example step, invent a small concrete scenario if you don't have a real one. |
| 15 | Rate each 1–5 for organization and fluency. |
| 10 | What's different about abstract topics for you vs. concrete ones? |

### Day 5 — Checkpoint

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 10 | Warm-up. |
| 25 | 2 unscripted 90-second answers, one concrete topic, one abstract. Record them and send to me. |
| 15 | Based on your weakest pattern, 2 focused reps on just that. |
| 10 | Compare to Day 1's baseline — what's visibly better? |

## Phase B — Precision (Days 6–10)

### Day 6 — Upgrading Vague Words

"Good," "thing," "stuff," "nice" carry no real information — the listener has to fill in the blank themselves, and often fills it in wrong. The fix isn't memorizing a thesaurus, it's catching the vague word right after you say it and swapping in something specific: what *kind* of good, what *specific* thing.

**Worked example.** "The presentation was good" → catch it → "the presentation was clear — she used one slide per point instead of cramming everything in." "It's a nice place" → catch it → "it's a quiet place, mostly empty on weekday mornings." Notice the upgrade isn't a fancier word, it's a more specific, truer one.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up. |
| 20 | Talk on a topic for 60 sec, then go back and upgrade 5 vague words to specific, truer ones. |
| 20 | Redo the same topic, trying to use the precise words the first time. |
| 15 | Which upgrades felt natural vs. forced? |

### Day 7 — Catching Yourself Mid-Sentence

When a sentence goes wrong mid-way — you realize the structure doesn't work, or you've contradicted yourself — don't freeze or push through the mess. Say "what I mean is—" and restart the clause clean. It sounds far more confident than either option, because it signals you're in control of your own speech, not lost in it.

**Worked example.** "So the reason I think we should — actually, the main issue isn't even that — what I mean is, the real problem is timing, not budget." Notice: no apology, no long explanation of the mistake, just a clean reset and forward motion.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up. |
| 20 | 3 topics. Whenever a sentence goes wrong, say "what I mean is—" and restart cleanly. |
| 20 | Identify your 2–3 most common slip patterns and drill fixing them specifically. |
| 15 | Did the reset phrase feel smoother than fighting the sentence? |

### Day 8 — Explaining Hard Things Simply

Same principle as the Feynman technique from Section 1: strip jargon, use plain language, and you'll immediately find the parts you don't actually understand yet — the jargon was hiding the gap.

**How to do it:** explain the concept as if to someone with zero background. Every time you reach for a technical term, stop and translate it into a plain-language version on the spot. If you can't translate it, that's the gap.

**Worked example.** Jargon version: "It's a hedge against downside volatility." Plain version, forced to translate: "It's basically insurance — if things go badly, this is designed to lose less, or even gain, to balance out the loss elsewhere."

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up. |
| 35 | Explain 3 concepts (trading, this course, anything complex) to an imagined beginner, 90 sec each — translate every jargon term on the spot as you hit it. |
| 20 | Where did jargon creep back in? What's the plain-language version? |

### Day 9 — Describing Vividly

One specific, concrete detail beats five vague ones. "The barista actually laughed out loud" does more work than "it was a funny situation" — the first gives the listener something to actually picture, the second gives them nothing.

**How to do it:** when describing something, find the one sensory detail — a specific sound, a specific visual, an exact phrase someone said — and use only that, instead of stacking general adjectives.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up. |
| 35 | Describe 4 things (object, place, person, memory) using one specific sensory detail each instead of general adjectives, 60–90 sec each. |
| 20 | Compare your most vivid description to your flattest — what was the actual difference? |

|---|---|

## Phase C — Opinion, Emotion, Self-Editing (Days 11–15)

### Day 11 — Stating Opinions Directly

Most people hedge opinions into mush — "I mean, I guess maybe kind of, I don't know, it could be okay?" State the opinion first, in one clean sentence, then justify it. You can still be open to being wrong without burying the actual opinion under six qualifiers before you've said anything.

**Worked example.** Hedged: "I don't know, I feel like maybe it's sort of not the best approach, but I could be wrong." Direct: "I don't think that's the best approach — here's why: [reason]. I'm open to being convinced otherwise, but that's where I land." Same openness to disagreement, but the actual position is stated clearly first.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up. |
| 35 | State and justify an opinion on 4 topics, 60–90 sec each: opinion first in one clean sentence, then the reason. |
| 20 | Where did you over-hedge? Could you have been more direct without being harsh? |

### Day 12 — Naming Emotions Precisely

"Fine," "weird," "annoyed" are vague defaults. Precise emotion words — frustrated vs. disappointed vs. anxious — call for different responses and land more honestly, because they're actually true instead of a placeholder.

**How to do it:** before you say the vague word, ask "precisely which flavor of that is this?" Frustrated (blocked from something) is different from disappointed (expected more) is different from anxious (worried about what's next).

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up. |
| 15 | List 10 vague emotion words you default to; pair each with 2 more precise alternatives. |
| 20 | Describe 3 real recent emotional moments using the precise words instead of the vague defaults. |
| 20 | Did precision make the description feel more honest? |

### Day 13 — Live Rephrasing

The skill from Day 7 (catching a bad sentence and resetting) now done live, on sentences you didn't write down first — the real test, since real conversation never gives you a script.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up. |
| 15 | Write 6 genuinely awkward sentences you tend to produce. |
| 25 | Say each out loud, then immediately clean-rephrase it out loud using "what I mean is—", no pause to write first. |
| 15 | Is live rephrasing faster than Day 3? |

### Day 14 — Rapid-Fire, Less Prep Time

Cutting prep time forces you to rely on the Point→Reason→Example→Point shape as a genuine reflex instead of something you have time to consciously plan out — which is the actual goal, since real conversation gives you no prep time at all.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up. |
| 30 | 6 topics — concrete, abstract, opinion, emotion mixed — 30 sec prep, 60 sec to answer each. |
| 15 | Rate each 1–5. What's your hardest topic type under time pressure? |
| 10 | What breaks first under pressure: organization, words, or confidence? |

### Day 15 — Checkpoint

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 10 | Warm-up. |
| 25 | An opinion (90 sec) + an emotional description (90 sec), minimal prep. Record and send. |
| 15 | Rep on weakest pattern. |

## Phase D — Argument & Delivery Range (Days 16–21)

### Day 16 — Structuring an Argument

A real argument needs all four parts spoken as one unit: **Claim → Reason → Evidence → Conclusion.** A claim with no evidence is just an assertion — "I think X" isn't an argument until you've said why and backed the why with something concrete.

**Worked example.** "We should delay the launch" (claim) "because the current version has a known bug in checkout" (reason) "— three testers hit it independently this week" (evidence) "so launching now risks losing customers right at the point of payment" (conclusion, restating and sharpening the claim).

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up. |
| 30 | 3 positions, full Claim-Reason-Evidence-Conclusion, 90 sec each. |
| 15 | Which part was weakest — reason, evidence, or conclusion? |
| 10 | Redo the weakest one. |

### Day 17 — Arguing Both Sides

Same four-part structure, deployed for a position you don't actually hold — this trains the skill of argument-building itself, separate from what you personally believe, which is what makes you persuasive rather than just repetitive.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up. |
| 40 | 3 topics — argue one side 60 sec (full Claim-Reason-Evidence-Conclusion), then immediately argue the other side 60 sec, same structure. |
| 15 | Which side was easier each time — genuine belief, or just familiarity? |

### Day 18 — Telling a Story Out Loud

A story needs shape: setup (who/where/what's normal, kept short) → complication (what changed, given most of the airtime) → resolution (how it landed, quick). Trim the setup ruthlessly, spend your time on the complication — see Section 8 for the full technique if you want more depth here.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up. |
| 30 | Tell 2 short real stories (90 sec each) with a short setup, a complication given most of the time, and a quick resolution. |
| 15 | Did you rush the ending or ramble the setup? |
| 10 | Redo the weaker one. |

### Day 19 — Tone and Pacing

Slow down at the important moment, speed through the connective bits. That contrast is what makes delivery sound alive instead of flat — a flat, even pace on every sentence, regardless of content, is what makes people sound like they're reading rather than talking.

**How to do it:** identify the one sentence in what you're about to say that matters most. Consciously slow down and add a small pause right before it. Speed normally through everything else.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up. |
| 20 | Say the same sentence 3 ways — neutral, enthusiastic, serious. Notice what actually changes: pace, emphasis, pitch. |
| 20 | Retell a Day 18 story, deliberately slowing at the key moment. |
| 15 | Which changes felt natural vs. performative? |

### Day 20 — Switching Registers

Same idea, different clothes: formal word choice and full sentences for professional contexts, contractions and casual phrasing for friends. The content doesn't change, the packaging does.

**Worked example.** Professional: "I wanted to follow up regarding the proposal — do you have availability this week to discuss?" Casual: "Hey, did you get a chance to look at that thing I sent? Want to hop on a call this week?" Same request, two different registers.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up. |
| 30 | Explain the same idea 3 times each way — professional, then casual. 6 short deliveries total. |
| 15 | What specifically changes between the two — word choice, sentence length, both? |
| 10 | Which register is more natural for you by default? |

### Day 21 — Checkpoint

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 10 | Warm-up. |
| 30 | Structure and deliver an argument (90 sec) + tell a short story (90 sec) + explain one idea in both registers. Record and send. |
| 20 | Full progress check against Day 15. |

## Phase E — Pressure and Exit (Days 22–24)

### Day 22 — Rapid Pressure Round

The test now is whether all the individual techniques (pause-and-structure, precise words, register control) still hold when you have almost no time to consciously apply them — that's the actual bar for "fluent," not just "capable when I have time to think."

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up. |
| 35 | 8 rapid prompts (opinion/explain/describe/argue mixed), 45 sec prep, 60 sec to answer, back to back. |
| 10 | Did quality hold across all 8, or degrade? |
| 10 | What's your recovery move when a response goes badly mid-round? |

### Day 23 — Real Conversation

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 10 | Plan one real 5-minute unscripted conversation or voice memo today, no prep. |
| 30 | Have it. |
| 10 | Note filler rate, any jumbling, one moment you're proud of. |
| 10 | How does this feel compared to Day 1's baseline? |

### Day 24 — Exit Check

One continuous 4-minute cold performance: explain a complex idea simply, argue a position and hold it under your own hardest counter-question, tell a short real story — back to back, minimal pause. Send it to me.

| Min | Do |
|---|---|
| 5 | Warm-up. |
| 40 | The full 4-minute performance, recorded. |
| 15 | Self-assess first, then compare to my evaluation. What changed most since Day 1? |

**Mastery bar:** organized, low-filler, confident delivery on unfamiliar topics, recovers smoothly from mistakes — not just on familiar ones. If you're not there yet, we repeat the weakest phase's drills on fresh material, not the whole section.
