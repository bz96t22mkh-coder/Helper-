# Module 13 — Working With Your Editor

## 1. Concept

Your editor turns your raw footage and intent into the final video — but they can only execute what you communicate. The skill here is **directing**, not editing: giving clear creative intent, organized materials, and specific feedback so your editor can consistently produce what's in your head without endless back-and-forth.

## 2. Why it matters

A great creator with poor editor communication produces inconsistent, slow-turnaround videos. A good communicator with an average editor often outperforms the reverse. This is a high-leverage relationship — invest in the process, not just the individual videos.

## 3. Communicating your vision

- Reference your brand one-pager (Module 2) and content pillar map (Module 3) with your editor once, up front, as standing context — don't re-explain your channel's identity every single video.
- For each video, communicate: the intended emotional arc (Module 9's structure), the planned beats/highlights (Module 4/5), and the specific tone (funny/tense/chaotic/analytical) per section — editors can't guess tone from raw footage alone.
- Explain jokes that depend on context your editor might not have (an inside community reference, a gaming-specific bit) — a joke edited without understanding the joke often gets cut or under-emphasized.

## 4. Providing footage efficiently

- Organize raw files clearly: consistent naming/folder structure by date and video, not a dumped folder of unlabeled clips.
- Use your verbal timestamp markers from filming (Module 12) as a first-pass guide — tell your editor explicitly which moments you flagged as strong.
- Include multiple takes only when necessary, clearly labeled — dumping unlabeled duplicate takes wastes editor time hunting for the usable one.

## 5. Communicating pacing and style

- Give reference videos (yours or others') for pacing/energy when starting the relationship or trying a new format — "cut like this" with a concrete example is far clearer than adjectives alone.
- Specify recurring style elements from your brand kit (Module 2): lower-third style, sound effect usage, caption/text style, transition style — establish these once as a style guide, not per-video.
- Be specific about what NOT to do too — e.g., "don't over-caption every line" or "don't add meme sound effects to genuine emotional moments" — negative examples prevent repeated misses.

## 6. Giving useful feedback (avoiding endless revisions)

- Give feedback in one consolidated pass with timestamps, not scattered messages sent as you think of them.
- Separate "this is wrong" (objective — a factual error, a missed instruction) from "this is a preference" (subjective — try a different transition) so your editor knows what's a hard fix vs. a style discussion.
- Explain *why* something isn't working, not just that it isn't — "the pacing drags here because there's no reaction shown for 8 seconds" is actionable; "this feels off" is not.
- Approve what's working, not just flag what isn't — editors need positive signal too, to know what to keep doing.

## 7. Developing a consistent editing style over time

Once you and your editor land on a style that performs well (check retention, Module 11), document it as a standing reference (part of your Module 24 playbook) so new videos default to that style without a fresh negotiation each time — freeing feedback rounds for genuinely new decisions instead of re-litigating settled ones.

## 8. Examples

- Instead of "make it funnier," give: "at 2:14 add emphasis (zoom/sound cue) on the line where I say [X] — that's the joke, it's currently underplayed."
- Instead of a folder of clips named IMG_0001–IMG_0047, provide: `2024-06-14_HorrorGame_EP3/` with the main session file and a short notes.txt listing flagged timestamps and what happens at each.

## 9. Editor brief template

```
VIDEO: ___
Format/pillar (per Module 3): ___
Emotional arc / tone per section: 
  Hook: ___
  Body segment 1: ___
  Body segment 2: ___
  Ending: ___
Flagged highlight timestamps (from filming markers): ___
Jokes/references needing context: ___
Specific style notes (captions, SFX, transitions): ___
What NOT to do: ___
Reference video(s) for pacing/tone: ___
Deadline: ___
```

## 10. Checklist

- [ ] Editor has standing access to brand one-pager and style guide (not re-explained per video)
- [ ] Raw footage organized and clearly named
- [ ] Highlight timestamps flagged from filming
- [ ] Feedback given in one consolidated, timestamped pass
- [ ] Feedback distinguishes objective fixes from subjective preferences

## 11. Common mistakes

- Giving vague feedback ("make it pop more") with no specifics or timestamps.
- Sending scattered feedback messages across multiple days instead of one consolidated pass, causing repeated re-renders.
- Never establishing a standing style guide, forcing the same creative decisions to be renegotiated every single video.
- Withholding context on jokes/references, leading the editor to cut or mis-time the exact moments meant to land hardest.

## 12. What beginners waste time on

- Trying to learn enough editing software to "just do it myself instead of explaining" — defeats the purpose of having an editor; invest in communication skill instead.
- Endless minor revision rounds on subjective taste calls instead of trusting a settled style guide and saving feedback rounds for real misses.

## 13. Homework

Write a complete Editor Brief for your next video and send it to your editor before they start cutting. After the first draft comes back, give one consolidated, timestamped feedback pass using the objective/subjective distinction from Section 6.

## 14. Checkpoint

You should be able to send a brief that requires no more than 1–2 feedback rounds to reach a publishable cut. If you're consistently needing 4+ rounds, the brief itself (not the editor) is likely the problem — revise your brief template with more specificity before the next video.
