### DAY 86 — REVENUE, EXPENSES & PROFIT AT THE BUSINESS LEVEL

**Module 15: Finance — Lesson 1**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Move from per-order numbers (which you've calculated since Module 6) to a whole-business monthly view — every order combined, plus the costs that exist at the business level, not the order level — and build a fully worked example you can trust.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. Two kinds of cost, and why the difference matters.** You already know **COGS** — what a specific order cost you to produce (blanks + branding + delivery, for that order). At the business level there's a second kind of cost that doesn't belong to any single order: **operating expenses** — things like marketing, phone/data, transport for sales visits, bank fees, software tools, sample garments bought for prospecting. These exist because the business exists, not because of any one sale.

**2. Business-level profit terms:**

| Term | Meaning at the business level |
|---|---|
| **Total revenue (monthly)** | The sum of everything customers paid you this month, across every order |
| **Total COGS (monthly)** | The sum of what every one of those orders actually cost to produce |
| **Gross profit (monthly)** | Total revenue − total COGS — same definition as per-order, just summed |
| **Operating expenses** | Business-level costs not tied to any single order (marketing, phone, transport, bank fees, tools, samples) |
| **Net profit** | Gross profit − operating expenses — what the business actually made this month, before tax |

Note: net profit here is a management number, for you to see how the business is doing — it is not the same as a tax return figure. What counts as deductible, and at what rate profit is taxed, is a real question for a professional (more on this on Day 90) — never assume this course's net profit figure is your tax liability.

**3. A fully worked monthly example.** Say this month you delivered and got paid on 5 orders:

| Order | Revenue | COGS | Gross profit | Margin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — security co., 60 shirts | R15,000 | R7,200 | R7,800 | 52.0% |
| 2 — cleaning co., 40 shirts | R9,800 | R5,400 | R4,400 | 44.9% |
| 3 — security co. reorder, 100 shirts | R24,000 | R13,500 | R10,500 | 43.8% |
| 4 — cleaning co., 25 overalls | R14,200 | R8,900 | R5,300 | 37.3% |
| 5 — security co., 90 shirts + jackets | R29,500 | R16,000 | R13,500 | 45.8% |
| **Total** | **R92,500** | **R51,000** | **R41,500** | **44.9%** |

Gross profit: R92,500 − R51,000 = **R41,500**. Blended gross margin: R41,500 ÷ R92,500 = **44.9%**.

Now the operating expenses for the same month:

| Expense | Amount |
|---|---|
| Marketing | R800 |
| Software/tools | R150 |
| Phone/data | R300 |
| Bank fees | R120 |
| Sample garments (prospecting) | R450 |
| **Total operating expenses** | **R1,820** |

**Net profit** = R41,500 − R1,820 = **R39,680**. **Net margin** = R39,680 ÷ R92,500 = **42.9%**.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

Notice each order's individual margin varies (37.3% to 52.0%) even though your pricing rules are consistent — that's normal, driven by things like discount tiers, product mix, or a particularly favorable/unfavorable supplier cost on that run. What matters at the business level is the *blended* number across all your orders combined, because that's what actually funds the business's operating costs and your own pay. A single low-margin order isn't a crisis; a consistently low blended margin across a whole month is a pricing problem worth revisiting (back in Module 6).

### DAY 87 — CASH FLOW & WORKING CAPITAL

**Module 15: Finance — Lesson 2**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Understand why cash flow — the timing of money in and out, not just the totals — gets tighter as you take on multiple overlapping orders, and see exactly why the deposit-first rule matters more, not less, as the business grows.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. Profit and cash are not the same thing.** A month can be profitable on paper (Day 86) while still running short on actual cash at some point during it, if money goes out (paying a supplier) before enough money has come in (collecting deposits and balances) to cover it. This gap is what "cash flow" management is about.

**2. Working capital**, plain language: the cash you need on hand right now to cover costs before you've been paid back for them. In this business, working capital risk shows up specifically when a customer's 50% deposit doesn't fully cover that order's COGS — meaning you need spare cash to bridge the gap until the balance arrives on delivery.

**3. A fully worked cash-flow timeline.** Say you start with a **R5,000 buffer** (this is exactly the kind of starting capital the roadmap recommends keeping in reserve), and three real orders overlap:

| Order | Revenue | Deposit (50%) | COGS | Balance (50%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | R20,000 | R10,000 | R11,000 | R10,000 |
| B | R15,000 | R7,500 | R7,200 | R7,500 |
| C | R30,000 | R15,000 | R17,500 | R15,000 |

Notice: Order A's deposit (R10,000) doesn't fully cover its COGS (R11,000) — a R1,000 shortfall that has to come from your buffer. Same for Order C: deposit R15,000 vs. COGS R17,500 — a R2,500 shortfall. Order B's deposit comfortably covers its COGS. This is normal and exactly why a working-capital buffer exists, even in a deposit-first model.

Here's the running cash balance as these three orders overlap in real time:

| Event | Change | Running balance |
|---|---|---|
| Start | — | R5,000 |
| Order A deposit received | +R10,000 | R15,000 |
| Pay supplier for Order A | −R11,000 | R4,000 |
| Order B deposit received | +R7,500 | R11,500 |
| Pay supplier for Order B | −R7,200 | R4,300 |
| Order A delivered, balance collected | +R10,000 | R14,300 |
| Order C deposit received | +R15,000 | R29,300 |
| Pay supplier for Order C | −R17,500 | R11,800 |
| Order B delivered, balance collected | +R7,500 | R19,300 |
| Order C delivered, balance collected | +R15,000 | R34,300 |

Final balance: **R34,300**. Check it against the totals: total revenue (R65,000) − total COGS (R35,700) = R29,300 gross profit; R5,000 starting buffer + R29,300 = R34,300 — matches exactly.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

The lowest point in that whole timeline was **R4,000**, right after paying Order A's supplier — still positive, comfortably above zero, but noticeably tighter than the R5,000 you started with, and this was with only three orders overlapping. This is exactly why the roadmap recommends **R3,000-R15,000 of starting capital** as a buffer: not to fund customer orders (the deposit does that), but to absorb the normal gap between a deposit and a full COGS payment when several orders are moving through the pipeline at once. As order volume grows, this buffer needs to grow with it — a business running 10 overlapping orders needs meaningfully more buffer than one running 3, even though the deposit-first rule is protecting you identically in both cases.

### DAY 89 — BREAK-EVEN ANALYSIS

**Module 15: Finance — Lesson 4**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Learn how to calculate the business's break-even revenue — the point where gross profit exactly covers fixed operating costs — and use it to see how close current performance already is to comfortably clearing it.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. What "break-even" means here.** Break-even revenue is the amount of monthly revenue at which your gross profit exactly equals your fixed operating costs — net profit is zero. Below that, the month runs at a loss; above it, every extra rand of gross profit is real net profit.

**2. The formula.** Break-even revenue = fixed monthly costs ÷ average gross margin (as a decimal). This works because your gross margin tells you what fraction of each rand of revenue becomes gross profit — so dividing your fixed costs by that fraction tells you exactly how much revenue is needed to generate enough gross profit to cover them.

**3. A fully worked example.** Say your real fixed monthly costs are:

| Fixed cost | Amount |
|---|---|
| Website hosting + domain (monthly share) | R100 |
| Business phone/data | R250 |
| Baseline marketing | R300 |
| Transport (sales visits, non-billed) | R500 |
| Bank fees | R120 |
| Admin/buffer | R230 |
| **Total fixed costs** | **R1,500** |

Using your business's typical blended gross margin from Day 86 (~45%): Break-even revenue = R1,500 ÷ 0.45 = **R3,333**.

That means you need roughly R3,333 of monthly revenue at a 45% blended margin just to cover fixed costs — well under the size of a single typical order (R15,000-R30,000, per Day 87's examples), meaning break-even itself is not the hard part of this business; consistency is.

**4. A second, more useful number: revenue needed for a real profit target.** Say you want the business to generate R15,000 of net profit in a month, on top of covering fixed costs: Revenue needed = (fixed costs + target profit) ÷ margin = (R1,500 + R15,000) ÷ 0.45 = R16,500 ÷ 0.45 = **R36,667**. At an average order size in the R15,000-R30,000 range, that's roughly one to two orders a month to hit that specific profit target — a concrete, useful planning number.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

Break-even is a floor, not a goal — it tells you the minimum before you're actually losing money, not what a healthy month looks like. The real planning value is the second calculation: working backward from an actual profit target to a revenue number, which turns "I want to earn more" into "I need roughly X more rands of orders this month," a number you can act on.

### EXERCISES

### DAY 90 — REINVESTMENT VS. OWNER-PAY POLICY

**Module 15: Finance — Lesson 5**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Set a simple, adjustable rule for splitting net profit between reinvesting in the business, paying yourself, and reserving for tax and buffer — so profit has a plan instead of just accumulating or disappearing with no structure.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. Why a rule beats deciding case by case.** Without a policy, every profitable month becomes a fresh decision about what to do with the money — easy to either underpay yourself indefinitely (burning out while "reinvesting everything") or overpay yourself early (starving the business of the buffer and growth capital it needs). A simple percentage split removes that decision fatigue and is easy to adjust deliberately as circumstances change.

**2. A starting-point split: 50/30/20.**
- **50% reinvestment** — marketing spend, buffer growth (Day 87 showed why this needs to scale with order volume), eventually your first hire (Module 18).
- **30% owner pay** — what you actually take out of the business for yourself.
- **20% tax reserve/buffer** — money you do not touch, set aside against your eventual tax obligation and unexpected costs.

**3. A worked example**, using Day 86's net profit figure of **R39,680**:
- Reinvestment (50%): R39,680 × 0.50 = **R19,840**
- Owner pay (30%): R39,680 × 0.30 = **R11,904**
- Tax reserve/buffer (20%): R39,680 × 0.20 = **R7,936**
- Check: 19,840 + 11,904 + 7,936 = **R39,680** ✓

**4. This split is a starting point, not a fixed law.** Early on, when the buffer from Day 87 is thin, tilting more toward reinvestment/buffer (e.g. 60/20/20) is reasonable. Once the business is consistently profitable with a healthy buffer, tilting more toward owner pay is reasonable too. Revisit the ratio deliberately every few months rather than drifting.

**5. The tax reserve is not optional, and it is not this course's job to tell you the rate.** Set aside a meaningful reserve every month you're profitable — but the actual applicable tax rate, threshold, and provisional tax obligations for your specific business structure (Module 3) need to be confirmed with a qualified accountant, and checked against the current published rates at **sars.gov.za** — never assume a rate from any course material, since these figures change and vary by your exact structure and income level. **[PROFESSIONAL]**, always.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

The point of a reinvestment/owner-pay split isn't the specific percentages — it's having a rule at all, applied consistently, that you can look at every month and immediately know what to do with the profit. A rule you actually follow beats a "perfect" rule you set once and ignore.
