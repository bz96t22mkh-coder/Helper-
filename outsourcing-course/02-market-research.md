### DAY 6 — Validating Real Demand

**Module 2: Market Research — Lesson 1**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Have a small number of short, low-pressure conversations with real prospects from your list — not to sell anything, but to find out whether the problem you think exists (uniform sourcing pain for security/cleaning companies) is actually a problem they recognize and feel.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. Validation is not selling.** A validation conversation has a different goal from a sales call: you're not trying to close anything, you're trying to learn whether your assumptions match reality. This matters because if you pitch too early, people tell you what you want to hear to end the conversation politely — you learn nothing real. If you ask genuine, curious questions about their current situation, you learn the truth, even when the truth is "we're happy with our current supplier" (which is useful information too).

**2. How to open a low-pressure conversation.** Keep it short — 2-3 minutes is a realistic ask from a busy owner or ops manager who doesn't know you yet. A workable structure over WhatsApp or a phone call:

- **Who you are, briefly, with no pitch:** "Hi, I'm [name] — I'm doing some research into how security/cleaning companies in [region] currently handle branded uniforms for their staff. Would you mind two quick questions?"
- **One question about their current situation:** "Who do you currently use for uniforms, and how's that working out?"
- **One question about pain, if any surfaces:** "What's the most annoying part of managing that — sizing, reorders, turnaround, cost, something else?"
- **A clean close, no pressure:** "That's really helpful, thank you — I'm not selling anything today, just researching." (If they show real interest, that's a bonus, not the goal — note it and follow up properly later, once your pricing and supplier relationships are actually ready in later modules.)

**3. What you're listening for.** Not a yes/no on "would you buy from me" — that question is too abstract and people say yes to be polite. Listen for *specific, current frustration*: a bad past experience, a slow turnaround they resent, sizing complaints from staff, a price they feel is too high, a supplier who's unreliable. Specific frustration is real signal. Vague politeness ("yeah it's fine") is not.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

You will get some no-replies and some "not interested" — that's normal and not a signal your business idea is wrong, it's a signal that cold outreach has a low response rate by nature. What matters at this stage is the quality of the handful of conversations you do get, not the percentage who respond. Don't let a quiet WhatsApp thread convince you the niche is wrong after only a few attempts — Module 9 and 10 build a real outreach volume and cadence; today is about *learning to listen*, at small scale, before you're doing it at real volume.

### EXERCISES

1. Write your own version of the opening message above, in your own voice — not copied word for word, since a templated-sounding message is an instant red flag to a business owner who gets spammed constantly.
2. Identify 5 prospects from your list you'll message or call today.
3. After each conversation (or non-response), log one line in `business/prospect-list.md`'s Notes column: what they said, or "no response."

(All open-ended — there's no fixed answer key. A strong outcome for today is 5 real attempts logged with honest notes, even if most are "no response" — that's real data, not a failure.)

### DAY 7 — Competitor Research

**Module 2: Market Research — Lesson 2**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Find and evaluate the local screen-printers and uniform suppliers already serving security and cleaning companies in your region, and extract real, usable lessons from their pricing, positioning, and turnaround — without copying them blindly.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. How to find real local competitors.** Use the same tools as your prospect research: Google ("uniform supplier [your city]", "workwear branding [your city]", "screen printing [your city]"), Google Maps, Facebook business pages, and ask a couple of your Day 6 contacts directly — "who do you currently use?" is often the fastest way to find a real, active competitor. You're looking specifically for suppliers who already serve B2B workwear clients, not general print shops that mostly do event t-shirts or once-off signage — the buying pattern and expectations are different.

**2. What to evaluate for each competitor you find.** Build a simple comparison using their public information (website, Facebook page, Google reviews, and if appropriate, a direct low-pressure enquiry as a "mystery shopper" asking for a quote like a real prospect would):

| What to check | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Pricing (if visible or quotable)** | Tells you the price ceiling and floor the market already accepts — critical input for Module 6 |
| **Positioning** | Do they lead with price, quality, speed, or relationship/service? This tells you what the market already competes on |
| **Turnaround time claims** | A benchmark for what customers already expect as "normal" |
| **Product range** | Do they specialize (workwear only) or do everything (also banners, signage, event shirts)? Specialists often have deeper supplier relationships in that category |
| **Reviews / reputation signals** | Recurring complaints in reviews are gaps you could position against; recurring praise tells you what actually matters to this market |
| **How they're found** | Google ranking, Facebook activity, referral-only — tells you where the market actually looks for suppliers, useful later for Module 16 (Marketing) |

**3. What NOT to do with this information.** Don't copy a competitor's pricing or positioning wholesale — you don't know their real cost structure, and undercutting blindly on price is exactly the underpricing mistake from Day 3. Use competitor research to understand the *landscape* (what's normal, what's missing, what's overpriced or underserved), not to set your own numbers directly — Module 6 builds your pricing from your own real costs.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

Finding "too many" competitors already in this space is not bad news — it confirms real, proven demand (nobody builds a uniform-printing business in a market with zero demand). What you're actually looking for is a **gap**: slow turnaround nobody's fixing, poor communication, a segment (e.g. smaller security companies) that bigger suppliers don't bother chasing because the orders are "too small" for them. That gap is where a new, attentive competitor — you — can realistically win business early, before you have scale or a long track record.

### EXERCISES

2. For each, write one sentence on what you'd learn from them (a strength worth understanding) and one sentence on a possible gap (a weakness you could realistically compete on as a beginner).

(Open-ended, using your own real research — never invent competitor names, prices, or reviews. If you can only find 1-2 real competitors in your specific area, that's real data too — note it, and note whether that's because demand is genuinely thin there or because it's simply underserved.)

### DAY 8 — Understanding Customer Problems

**Module 2: Market Research — Lesson 3**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Go beneath the surface-level "yeah uniforms are fine" response and understand the real, recurring problems security and cleaning company owners have with uniforms — using better, more specific questions than Day 6's quick opener.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. The likely real problems in this niche, based on how these businesses actually operate** (from the roadmap's niche analysis) — treat these as hypotheses to confirm with your own conversations, not assumed facts:

- **Staff turnover replacement pain.** Security and cleaning companies have high staff turnover (guards and cleaners leave and get replaced often) — every new hire needs a uniform, often urgently, often as a single small order that's annoying for a supplier with a high MOQ to bother with quickly.
- **Budget sensitivity.** These are often SME businesses operating on thin margins themselves, competing for contracts partly on cost — uniform spend is a real line item they watch closely, not a "nice to have."
- **Brand consistency across a growing team.** A company with guards or cleaners across multiple client sites needs everyone looking consistent and professional — a client company itself may complain if uniforms look mismatched or worn out, since guards/cleaners represent that client's premises to the public.
- **Reliability and turnaround anxiety.** A company can't have staff without uniforms standing idle, or worse, at a client site out of uniform — so a supplier who's slow or unreliable creates a real operational problem for them, not just an inconvenience.

**2. Scripted validation questions that surface real problems** (use naturally in conversation, don't read them like a survey):

- "When someone new joins your team, how long does it typically take to get them properly uniformed?"
- "Have you ever had a client comment on how your staff look, good or bad?"
- "What happens when you need just 2-3 replacement shirts, not a big order — is that easy or annoying to sort out?"
- "Has a uniform supplier ever let you down on timing? What happened?"
- "Roughly, is uniform cost something you think about a lot, or is it a small line item you don't worry much about?"

These are open questions that invite a story, not a yes/no — stories are where the real, specific pain shows up.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

The goal today is not to confirm every hypothesis above — it's entirely possible some don't hold in your specific area or with your specific contacts, and a few might reveal a problem you hadn't considered at all. Treat every one of today's hypotheses as something to test, not something to confirm no matter what you hear. Real research sometimes tells you your assumptions were wrong — that's a good outcome, not a bad one, because it's much cheaper to learn that now than after you've built pricing and supplier relationships around a wrong assumption.

### EXERCISES

1. Have at least 3 more real conversations (building on Day 6's contacts, or new ones) using the scripted questions above, adapted to sound natural in your own voice.
2. For each conversation, note which of the four hypothesized problems (turnover pain, budget sensitivity, brand consistency, reliability anxiety) came up, unprompted or in response to your questions, and how strongly.
3. Write a short summary: based on real conversations so far (Day 6 + Day 8 combined), which 1-2 problems seem most real and most common in your specific market?

(Open-ended, using your own real research. Do not invent quotes or responses — if you didn't get enough real conversations today, say so honestly and note it as unfinished work carried into Day 9.)

### DAY 9 — Module 2 Capstone: Confirming Your Niche

**Module 2: Market Research — Lesson 4**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Formally confirm your starting niche — not by re-reading the roadmap's recommendation, but by scoring it against your own real research from Days 6-8, using a simple weighted decision framework, and logging the final decision.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**A simple weighted decision framework.** This is a standard business tool for turning "it feels right" into a documented, defensible decision: list the factors that matter, weight each by importance (weights across all factors should add to 1.0, or you can use a simple 1-5 importance scale per factor), score each option against each factor, multiply, and total.

**Illustrative example of the method** (using placeholder scores — you'll do this with your own real research below, not these numbers):

| Factor | Weight | Security co. score (1-5) | Weighted | Cleaning co. score (1-5) | Weighted |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Demand you actually found | 0.25 | 4 | 1.00 | 4 | 1.00 |
| Ease of getting a real response | 0.20 | 4 | 0.80 | 3 | 0.60 |
| Problem severity you heard | 0.25 | 5 | 1.25 | 3 | 0.75 |
| Competition/gap you found | 0.15 | 3 | 0.45 | 4 | 0.60 |
| Repeat-order potential (turnover-driven) | 0.15 | 4 | 0.60 | 4 | 0.60 |
| **Total** | **1.00** | | **4.10** | | **3.55** |

(Weighted score = weight × raw score, summed. Here security scores higher overall — 4.10 vs 3.55 — because it scored higher on the two factors weighted most heavily. This is a placeholder illustration only; your actual weights and scores must come from what you genuinely found in Days 6-8, not this example.)

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

This framework doesn't replace judgment — it forces your judgment to be explicit and checkable, so if a decision turns out wrong later, you can see exactly which assumption was off, rather than wondering vaguely "why didn't this work." It's also fine, and honest, if your own real numbers point somewhere different from the roadmap's general recommendation — for example, if your specific area has thin cleaning-company demand but strong security-company response — that's your research overriding a general recommendation with local evidence, which is exactly what this exercise is for. If your findings genuinely support running both niches roughly equally (as the roadmap originally suggested), that's a valid outcome too — the point is that it's now a decision built on your evidence, not an assumption.

### EXERCISES

2. Score security companies and cleaning companies (and, if relevant, note any other niche that surfaced unexpectedly during your conversations) against your weighted factors.
3. State your final niche decision in one sentence, referencing the strongest piece of real evidence behind it.
