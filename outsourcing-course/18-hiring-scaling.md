### DAY 103 — WHEN TO HIRE

**Module 18: Hiring & Scaling — Lesson 1**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Replace "hire when revenue hits some number" with a concrete, checkable readiness test — and run that test honestly against your actual business as it stands today.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. Why a revenue target is bad hiring advice.** Two businesses can hit the same monthly revenue and need completely different things. One has a founder buried in re-typing WhatsApp orders into the CRM every night; the other has automation (Module 12) handling that same volume with the founder only touching the phone for actual sales calls. Revenue tells you the business is working. It tells you nothing about where *your* time is currently going, which is the only thing hiring actually fixes.

**2. The three-part readiness test.** Hiring makes sense only when all three are true at once:

- **Capacity signal** — there is a real, recurring backlog: orders sitting untouched longer than they should, follow-ups going out late or not at all, leads going cold because nobody responded within a reasonable window, quotes taking days instead of hours to go out. One busy week doesn't count. A pattern across several weeks does.
- **Definability signal** — the task causing the backlog is describable step by step, with no judgment call that only you can make. "Chase suppliers for order status and update the CRM" is definable. "Decide whether to give this difficult customer a discount to save the account" is not — that stays [ME] regardless of how busy you are.
- **Affordability signal** — your business can carry the added monthly cost for at least three months, even in a slower month, without touching money set aside for supplier deposits or your own basic living costs. This is a finance-tracker question (Module 15), not a gut-feel one: look at your actual average monthly gross profit over the last few months, not your best month.

If any one of the three is missing, you're not ready yet — you're either not busy enough (nothing to hand off), the busyness is all judgment-work (nothing *can* be handed off), or you can't sustain the cost (hiring would create a new cash-flow risk, which the roadmap already flagged as the single biggest way a business like this fails).

**3. What hiring too early looks like.** You bring someone on before a task is well-defined. They ask you questions constantly because the process only exists in your head. You end up spending more time explaining and correcting than you saved. This is the single most common reason a first hire feels like "it didn't work out" — the problem was usually that the task wasn't ready to be handed off, not that the person was wrong.

**4. What hiring too late looks like.** You keep absorbing the backlog personally. Quality slips because you're rushing. Sales calls get pushed back because admin is eating your hours — and sales is the one thing in this business that only you can do right now. Growth flattens out not because demand stopped, but because your personal hours became the ceiling.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

The test is a triangle, not a checklist you tick in order — all three points have to hold at the same time. A business that's busy and can afford help but where the busy work is all judgment calls (say, difficult supplier negotiations) isn't ready to hire; it's ready to get better at those negotiations, or to raise prices so there's less volume to negotiate over. A business drowning in pure admin but with three months of savings and no cushion beyond that also isn't ready — hiring there just moves the cash-flow risk from "too busy" to "can't make payroll." Get comfortable saying "not yet" to yourself here. It's a cheaper mistake than hiring too early.

### EXERCISES

These are about your real business, so there's no fixed answer key — here's what a strong answer looks like for each.

1. **List every task you did in a typical week recently**, with a rough hours estimate next to each. Mark each one Judgment (only you can decide this) or Non-judgment (repeatable, describable, no unique call to make). A strong answer is specific — "chasing the embroiderer for order status" rather than "supplier stuff" — because vague tasks can't pass the definability signal even if they're genuinely non-judgment.
2. **Total your non-judgment hours for the week.** A strong answer treats this number honestly: if it's under 3-4 hours, hiring won't meaningfully free you up yet, no matter how appealing it sounds. If it's a recurring 8+ hours, that's a real signal.
3. **Check your last 3 months of gross profit in your finance tracker** (Module 15) and ask: could this business absorb an extra R2,500-R6,000/month — a realistic South African part-time VA range you'll size properly on Day 104 — for three straight months, including a deliberately bad one, without touching deposit money or your own baseline living costs? A strong answer uses your worst recent month, not your best, to stress-test this.
4. **Name one task on your list you're tempted to hand off that is actually a judgment call in disguise.** (Common trap: "follow up with a customer who's unhappy" sounds like admin but is really a relationship-repair judgment call.) A strong answer catches at least one of these and explains why it stays [ME].

### DAY 104 — WHO TO HIRE FIRST

**Module 18: Hiring & Scaling — Lesson 2**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Compare the three realistic first hires for a business like this — virtual assistant, salesperson, operations/fulfilment coordinator — and work out which one actually fits your current bottleneck, using yesterday's task list as evidence instead of instinct.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. The three candidates.**

| Role | What they'd take off you | Typical SA cost range | Ramp time |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Virtual assistant (VA)** | Admin, lead research, CRM data entry, drafting (not sending) quotes, invoice chase-ups, calendar/follow-up scheduling | R2,500-R6,000/month part-time (15-20 hrs/week), or R80-R150/hour | Days, since tasks are narrow and well-defined |
| **Salesperson** | Discovery calls, quoting, closing | Base + commission, meaningfully higher fixed cost, plus ramp time to learn your product and pitch | Weeks to months — needs a proven, repeatable process to learn from |
| **Operations/fulfilment coordinator** | Chasing suppliers, scheduling QC, packaging, delivery coordination, order-tracker updates | Similar range to a VA, but needs product/process familiarity | Weeks — needs to learn your suppliers and QC standard |

**2. Why a VA is almost always the right first hire in this business.** Three reasons stack together:

- **Lowest risk.** The tasks are narrow, well-defined (assuming you did Day 103 properly), and don't touch the relationships that make or break a customer. If it goes wrong, you lose a small amount of money and time, not a customer.
- **Fastest payback.** A VA doesn't need to learn your sales pitch or your supplier relationships to be useful in week one. Give them a task brief (Day 105) and they can start producing value almost immediately.
- **Frees the two things only you can do.** Every hour a VA takes off your admin load is an hour that goes back into sales conversations and quality inspection — the two [ME]-tagged activities the roadmap has flagged as non-negotiable since Day 1. A VA doesn't replace you at either; it protects your time for both.

**3. Why a salesperson usually comes later, not first.** It's tempting — "hire someone to do sales so I don't have to" — but for a business built on zero track record and relationship trust (which is exactly this business, per the roadmap's own risk list), that backfires for two reasons: a new salesperson has none of your credibility or relationships yet, and there's no proven, scripted process (Module 9) mature enough to hand to someone else without you having run it yourself for months first. Hire a salesperson once your own sales process is repeatable enough that you could hand someone a script and a target list and reasonably expect results — not before.

**4. Why an ops/fulfilment coordinator usually comes third, not second.** This role only earns its cost once fulfilment volume itself — not lead volume, not sales capacity — is the actual bottleneck: real orders piling up faster than you can personally chase suppliers, run QC, and coordinate delivery. For most businesses at this stage, that bottleneck shows up after sales capacity has already been solved, which means it's rarely the first hire.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

The order — VA, then salesperson or ops coordinator depending on which becomes the real constraint — isn't a rule to follow blindly. It follows directly from Day 103's readiness test: a VA is almost always the first role to clear all three signals (capacity: admin backlog is common early; definability: admin tasks are the easiest to describe precisely; affordability: it's the cheapest of the three) simply because of what kind of work it takes off your plate. If your honest Day 103 answer showed your bottleneck is something else entirely — say, you have more qualified leads than you can personally call — the framework still applies, just pointing somewhere else. Use the evidence, not the assumption.

### EXERCISES

1. **Using yesterday's non-judgment task list, sort each task into which of the three roles would most naturally own it.** A strong answer notices that most non-judgment tasks land under VA — that's expected, and it's the signal, not a mistake in your sorting.
2. **A friend tells you: "Hire a salesperson first so you don't have to do sales yourself."** Explain in 2-3 sentences why this is risky advice for your specific business at this stage.
   **Check your work:** A strong answer names both problems — a new hire has no track record or relationships with your niche's buyers yet, and you don't have a proven, scripted sales process mature enough to hand off without having run it yourself first. Selling in this business currently depends on trust you personally built; a stranger can't inherit that on day one.
3. **Which of the three roles is closest to clearing all three Day 103 signals for your actual business right now?** Name it and justify with your real numbers, not a hypothetical.

### DAY 105 — DELEGATING TO A VIRTUAL ASSISTANT

**Module 18: Hiring & Scaling — Lesson 3**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Learn to hand off work in a way a VA can actually execute without you hovering over them — what to delegate first, how to write a task brief that removes ambiguity, and how to check the work without turning into a full-time manager.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. What to hand off first.** Start with the lowest-risk, most repeatable, most clearly defined task on your list — not the biggest time-saver. A good starting task:

- Has a clear, checkable "done" state (the lead is in the CRM with all required fields filled, not "the lead is handled").
- Doesn't touch money, pricing decisions, or a customer relationship directly.
- Can be checked in under two minutes by glancing at the result.

For this business, the classic first hand-offs are: **lead research** (finding and logging new prospects into the CRM with the fields your prospect-list/CRM template already defines), **CRM data entry and hygiene** (logging inbound WhatsApp/email leads, updating stage, flagging stale ones), and **invoice/payment follow-up using a template you approve** (sending a pre-written reminder message, not negotiating). Notice none of these require closing a sale or making a pricing call — they clear the same definability bar from Day 103.

**2. How to write a task brief a VA can actually execute.** The single biggest cause of delegation failure isn't a bad hire — it's a vague brief. A usable brief has five parts:

- **Objective** — the outcome in one sentence ("Find 20 new security-company prospects in [city] and add them to the CRM").
- **Exact steps** — numbered, in order, with no missing step assumed obvious. Include *where* to look, *what fields* to fill, and *what format*.
- **Done looks like** — a specific, checkable end state ("20 rows added, every required CRM field filled, no duplicates of existing prospects").
- **What NOT to do** — the boundary. ("Do not contact anyone. Do not mark any lead as qualified — that's a judgment call, flag it for me instead.")
- **What to do if stuck** — a named person (you) and a channel, so they don't guess or stall.

A brief missing any of these five will produce inconsistent work even from a genuinely capable person — that's not a management failure on their part, it's a brief failure on yours.

**3. How to check work without micromanaging.** Micromanaging means watching every step. Checking means sampling the output against the "done looks like" line. Practically: review 100% of the work for the first few batches (to catch brief gaps early, not performance gaps), then drop to spot-checking a sample once quality is consistently correct for a couple of weeks running. If you find an error, ask first whether the brief was ambiguous before assuming the person did something wrong — usually it's the brief.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

A task brief is really just an SOP for one person, written once and reused every time you hand off that task again — which is exactly the discipline Module 19 builds out formally for the whole business next. Getting good at writing one now, on a single task, is the smallest possible version of the skill you'll need at full scale.

### EXERCISES

1. **Pick one non-judgment task from your Day 103 list and write a full five-part task brief for it**, as if handing it to a stranger with zero context on your business.
   A strong answer: someone with no prior knowledge of your niche, your CRM, or your prospect list could follow it and produce the correct result without asking you a single clarifying question. If you can picture a question they'd likely ask, that's a gap in the brief — fix it before moving on.
2. **Identify the one line in your brief that removes the most ambiguity.** A strong answer usually points to either the "done looks like" line or the "what NOT to do" line — these are the two most commonly missing in a first-draft brief.

### DAY 106 — THE FULL DECISION FRAMEWORK: AUTOMATE, DELEGATE, OR KEEP

**Module 18: Hiring & Scaling — Lesson 4**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Apply one consistent decision framework — automate it, delegate it, or keep doing it yourself — to every recurring task across the whole business you've built through Module 17, and come out with a real, current map of where your time should actually be going.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. The three-way test, in order.** For any recurring task, ask, in this order:

1. **Can it run with zero human step, reliably, once built?** → **[AUTOMATION]**. This is always the first question, not the last — an automated task costs you nothing ongoing once it's working, so it beats delegation whenever it's genuinely possible. The roadmap already automated several of these in Module 12: outreach and follow-up reminders, quote calculation, invoice generation, reorder reminders, reporting.
2. **If not, does it require your specific judgment, relationships, or trust?** → **[ME]**, always. This never changes regardless of how busy you get — the sales conversation and close, supplier negotiation, final quality inspection, and judgment calls on difficult customers stay yours, per the roadmap's own rule.
3. **If it's neither automatable nor judgment-dependent, but it's repeatable and describable** → **[EMPLOYEE]**, once Day 103's affordability signal clears. Until then, it's still [ME] by default — not because it should be, but because there's no one else yet.

**2. Why the order matters.** Automation before delegation isn't a preference, it's cost logic: an automation, once built and tested, runs at effectively zero marginal cost forever. A delegated task keeps costing money and needs ongoing checking, every time it runs. Reaching for a hire before checking whether the task could just be automated is the most common way founders overspend on headcount they didn't need.

**3. This is a living map, not a one-time exercise.** The correct classification for a given task changes over time — something that's [ME] today because volume is too low to justify automating it can become [AUTOMATION] once volume justifies the build effort, and something [AUTOMATION] can later need an [EMPLOYEE] layer on top once it produces more output (like leads) than you can personally act on. Re-running this exercise periodically (quarterly is reasonable) is part of running the business, not a one-off Day 106 event.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

The framework only works if you classify tasks honestly rather than by what feels good. It's tempting to leave everything [ME] because you trust yourself most, or to over-automate things that genuinely need judgment because automation feels more "grown up" than admitting you still need to personally handle something. The tag isn't a status symbol — [ME] on the sales close is exactly as correct, permanently, as [AUTOMATION] on a reorder reminder.

### EXERCISES

This is a full review exercise across your real business — there's no single correct answer sheet, but here's what a strong pass looks like.

For each, apply the three-way test and tag it [AUTOMATION] / [ME] / [EMPLOYEE].
   **What a strong pass looks like:** every task gets exactly one primary tag with a one-line reason; tasks already automated in Module 12 are correctly left as [AUTOMATION] rather than second-guessed; the sales close, supplier negotiation, final QC, and difficult-customer judgment calls are all still [ME] with no exceptions; anything currently [ME] purely because you haven't gotten around to building or delegating it is honestly flagged as such, not disguised as a permanent judgment call.
2. **Find at least one task you're currently doing yourself that shouldn't be** — either it should already be automated given what Module 12 built, or it's now a strong candidate for your Day 104-105 VA hand-off. Name it and say which direction it should move.

### DAY 107 — CAPSTONE: YOUR WRITTEN HIRING PLAN

**Module 18: Hiring & Scaling — Lesson 5**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Turn five days of evidence into one short, real, written hiring plan you can act on when the moment is right — then confirm you've genuinely absorbed this module before moving to Module 19.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

A hiring plan for a small business doesn't need to be a formal document — it needs to answer five questions clearly enough that you (or a future you, months from now) can act on it without redoing this week's thinking:

1. **Next hire** — which role, based on Days 103-104's evidence.
2. **When** — the specific readiness signals you're watching for, not a calendar date (revisit Day 103's triangle).
3. **Budget range** — a real number from your finance tracker's actual margins, not a guess.
4. **What stays automated** — pulled straight from Day 106's classification.
5. **What stays delegated once hired** — the task brief(s) already written or queued from Day 105.

Writing this down now, while the thinking is fresh, means the actual hiring decision later is a five-minute check against a plan instead of a from-scratch debate.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

A hiring plan is a decision-support document, not a commitment. Writing "next hire: VA, when weekly non-judgment hours exceed 6 for a month straight" doesn't obligate you to hire the moment that happens — it obligates you to *notice* when it happens instead of drifting past the signal because you're heads-down running the business. That noticing is the entire value of the exercise.

   **Check your work:**
