### DAY 81 — COMPLAINT-HANDLING FRAMEWORK

**Module 14: Customer Service — Lesson 1**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Learn a simple, repeatable framework for handling any complaint professionally, and build your own real script from it — so you're never improvising tone under pressure.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. The framework: Listen, Acknowledge, Fix, Follow up (LAFF).**

- **Listen** — let the customer fully explain the problem before you respond. Don't interrupt with an explanation or a defense, even if you already know what happened. Being heard, first, defuses more anger than any fast solution does.
- **Acknowledge** — name the problem back to them and say plainly that it's not okay, without being defensive or making excuses. "You're right, the logo placement on 10 of those shirts is inconsistent — that shouldn't have gone out like that."
- **Fix** — offer a concrete resolution, not a vague promise. State exactly what you'll do and by when (this connects directly to Day 83's redo-vs-refund decision tree).
- **Follow up** — after the fix is delivered, check back in to confirm it actually resolved things. This step is the one beginners skip most, and it's what separates "we fixed it" from "we fixed it and made sure you're actually satisfied."

**2. Tone, throughout.** Calm, direct, and taking real ownership — never defensive, never blaming the supplier or "bad luck" in front of the customer even if that's technically the cause. The customer's relationship is with you, not your supplier; you absorb that and handle your supplier relationship separately.

**3. A real example script**, using LAFF on a logo-placement complaint:

> **Customer:** "Half these shirts have the logo off-center — this isn't what we agreed on."
> **You (Listen):** "Tell me more — is it all of them or specific ones? And how far off is it?"
> *[Customer explains: roughly 10 of 60 shirts, logo noticeably higher and to the left compared to the rest.]*
> **You (Acknowledge):** "You're right to flag that — the logo should be consistent across every shirt, and it sounds like about 10 weren't. That's on us, not something you should have to accept."
> **You (Fix):** "I'll get those 10 replaced at no cost to you. I can have corrected ones to you by [specific date] — would that work, or do you need them sooner for a specific reason?"
> **You (Follow up, a few days after delivery of the replacements):** "Just checking in — did the replacement shirts land properly and match the rest of the order?"

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

A complaint handled well often strengthens a relationship rather than damaging it — the customer learns you take ownership and fix things properly, which is exactly the trust a repeat B2B account needs. A complaint handled defensively or slowly does the opposite, even if the original mistake was small. The size of the original problem matters less than the quality of the response.

### EXERCISES

1. **Rewrite the example script above in your own words and tone** — same LAFF structure, but sounding like you, not a template. A strong answer keeps all four steps distinct and doesn't skip straight to "Fix" without acknowledging first.
2. **Draft a second script for a different scenario**: a customer says the shirts feel like a cheaper fabric than the sample they were shown. A strong answer still follows LAFF, even though this complaint is murkier (fabric perception is more subjective than a visible logo error) — Listen and Acknowledge don't require you to have already decided you're at fault, just that you're taking the concern seriously.

### DAY 82 — DELAYS: PROACTIVE COMMUNICATION SOP

**Module 14: Customer Service — Lesson 2**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Build a simple SOP for telling a customer about a delay before they have to ask you about it — because how a delay is communicated matters more to most B2B customers than the delay itself.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. Why "before they ask" is the whole point.** A customer who finds out about a delay because they chased you feels ignored, even if the delay itself was completely reasonable (a supplier running late, a branding issue). The exact same delay, communicated proactively, reads as "this supplier is on top of things" instead of "this supplier went quiet on me." The information is identical — only the timing and initiative differ, and that difference is the entire relationship impact.

**2. When to notify.** As soon as you know — the moment your supplier tells you production is running behind, or your own QC catches something that'll push delivery back, that's when you tell the customer, not the day the original delivery date arrives and passes silently.

**3. What the message should include:**
- What's delayed and by roughly how long (a real estimate, not "not sure yet" if you can help it)
- A brief, honest reason (doesn't need excessive detail — "our decorator had a production delay on this batch" is enough)
- The new expected date
- An offer to answer questions or adjust if the new date is a real problem for them

**4. Example proactive delay message:**

> "Quick update on your order — our decorator had a production delay on this batch, so instead of [original date] we're now looking at [new date], about [X] days later. I'll confirm again as soon as it's through QC on my end. Let me know if that new date causes any issues on your side and we'll figure something out."

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

Overpromising delivery dates before confirming real supplier lead times is already flagged as a classic beginner mistake (roadmap section 8) — this SOP is the recovery plan for the delays that happen anyway, even with good supplier management. Being proactive doesn't erase a delay, but it protects the trust that a silent, discovered delay would cost you for free.

### EXERCISES

1. **Write your own version of the proactive delay message** for a realistic scenario (pick a plausible cause: supplier delay, branding rework, logistics issue). A strong answer includes all four elements above and reads as calm and in-control, not apologetic-to-the-point-of-alarming.
2. **Scenario: you don't yet know exactly how long the delay will be** — only that it's happening. Do you wait until you have an exact date before telling the customer? A strong answer says no: notify as soon as you know a delay is likely, with your best estimate and a promise to confirm the exact date shortly — silence while you wait for certainty costs you more than an approximate update does.

2. **[ME]** Commit to a personal rule: any delay you become aware of gets communicated to the affected customer within the same working day, not "when I get a chance."
3. **[AUTOMATION]** No new automation needed today — this stays a judgment-and-relationship task, permanently [ME], per the Module 12 approval rule (it's a customer-facing message).

### DAY 83 — WRONG OR DAMAGED ORDERS: RESOLUTION SOP

**Module 14: Customer Service — Lesson 3**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Build a clear resolution SOP, including a redo-vs-refund decision tree, for when an order reaches a customer wrong or damaged despite QC — so you're deciding with a framework, not improvising under pressure.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. First, apply LAFF (Day 81) regardless of which resolution you land on** — listen and acknowledge before you even get to deciding redo vs. refund.

**2. The redo-vs-refund decision tree.** Work through these questions in order:

```
Is the issue affecting the WHOLE order or PART of it?
  ├─ Whole order (wrong garment type, wrong branding entirely)
  │    → Is a corrected redo possible within a timeframe the customer
  │      actually needs?
  │         ├─ Yes → REDO the full order at no cost, on an expedited
  │         │        timeline if possible
  │         └─ No (they need it sooner than a redo allows, or trust
  │                is too damaged) → FULL REFUND, and separately offer
  │                to make it right on a future order if the
  │                relationship is worth preserving
  │
  └─ Part of the order (some units, not all)
       → How many units, and can they function without those units
         short-term?
            ├─ Small % of order, customer can wait → REDO just the
            │    affected units at no cost (this is the most common
            │    case — see Day 81's logo example)
            └─ Large % of order, or they can't wait at all →
                 treat as whole-order case above; consider a PARTIAL
                 REFUND on the affected portion plus an expedited
                 redo for the rest
```

**3. Whose fault it is affects your supplier conversation, not the customer's resolution.** Whether the mistake was your QC missing something, or your supplier's error that slipped through, the customer gets the same resolution either way — they didn't cause the problem and shouldn't be part of untangling whose fault it was. You separately follow up with your supplier (possibly a cost recovery conversation) after the customer is already taken care of.

**4. Timing matters as much as the decision.** A redo or refund offered within the same conversation as the complaint (per LAFF's "Fix" step) lands completely differently than the same offer delivered two days later after you "thought about it."

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

The decision tree exists so "how big is the mistake" — not "how do I feel today" or "how much will this cost me" — drives the resolution. A consistent, fair process protects you from both under-reacting (losing a customer over a fixable mistake you were slow to fix) and over-reacting (refunding a whole order over an issue a small redo would have solved just as well).

### EXERCISES

1. **Run three scenarios through the decision tree** and state the resolution for each: (a) 3 of 40 shirts have visible stitching defects, customer has 3 weeks before they need them; (b) the entire order arrived in the wrong garment color, customer needs them in 4 days for a client site visit; (c) 15 of 100 shirts are the wrong size, customer says they can manage short-term with existing stock. A strong answer applies the tree correctly to each: (a) redo the 3 units; (b) likely a refund (or an extremely expedited redo only if genuinely achievable in 4 days, which is unlikely) plus an offer to make it right on the reorder; (c) redo the 15 wrong-size units, since the customer explicitly said they can wait.
2. **Explain in one sentence why the customer's resolution shouldn't depend on whose fault the mistake was** (yours vs. your supplier's). A strong answer notes the customer's contract is with you, not your supplier — that internal accountability question is yours to resolve afterward, separately.

### BUSINESS IMPLEMENTATION

1. **[ME]** Adopt the decision tree as your standard process for the next wrong/damaged order, real or not.
2. **[ME]** Document your real supplier-side follow-up process (who you'd contact, what you'd ask for — cost recovery, faster turnaround on the redo) separately from the customer resolution.
3. **[PROFESSIONAL]** For any refund large enough to have real tax/accounting implications, note it correctly in your finance tracker (built in Module 15) rather than just handling it informally.

### DAY 84 — LATE PAYMENTS: STAGED ESCALATION SOP

**Module 14: Customer Service — Lesson 4**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Formalize the late-payment escalation process, building directly on Day 78's payment-collection SOP, as a complete customer-service policy you apply consistently.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

This SOP is the same four-stage structure introduced in Module 13's payment collection SOP (Day 78) — today, treat it as a full customer-service policy in its own right, since a late payment is fundamentally a customer-relationship problem, not just an accounting one.

**1. Reminder (friendly).** Assume good faith — most late payments are simple admin delay, not refusal. Warm, short, specific about the amount owed.

**2. Firm reminder (professional).** Restate the original agreed terms plainly. Ask directly for a payment date. No apologizing for asking — you delivered what was agreed; being paid on the agreed terms isn't an imposition.

**3. Hold future orders.** State this plainly and actually do it — this is the point where "customer service" and "protecting the business" are the same action. A customer who's not paying for a completed order shouldn't be able to place a new one.

**4. Last resort — [PROFESSIONAL].** A formal written demand, and if that doesn't resolve it, a small-claims or debt-collection process. This is where you stop handling it informally and get qualified help — trying to "solve" a genuinely stuck non-payment situation yourself past this point usually costs more time and stress than it's worth.

**2. What stays constant across every stage: respect.** Even stage 4 doesn't mean hostility — it means moving from a direct conversation to a formal process. You can be completely firm about wanting payment without ever being rude, and firmness (not rudeness) is what actually gets results while keeping the door open to the relationship recovering later.

**3. A note on "what if a customer just won't pay."** Some non-payment situations are unrecoverable, and that's a real possible outcome, not a failure of your process — it's exactly why the deposit-first rule caps your downside to the unpaid balance on goods you've already been partially paid for, never the full order. Accept that outcome when it happens, hold future orders permanently for that account, and move on rather than letting one bad account consume disproportionate time and energy.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

Chasing a late payment respectfully is a skill, not an awkward chore to avoid — and avoiding it (going quiet, hoping they'll remember on their own) is worse for the relationship than a clear, professional reminder would have been. Customers generally respect a business that's organized and direct about money more than one that seems unsure whether it's allowed to ask.

### EXERCISES

1. **Draft your stage 3 "holding future orders" message** — this is usually the hardest one to write because it's the first stage that's genuinely consequential. A strong answer states the hold plainly and factually, ties it directly to the specific unpaid amount, and leaves the door open ("happy to resume as soon as this is settled") without sounding like a threat.
2. **Scenario: a customer at stage 2 replies asking for a two-week extension, citing a genuine cash-flow issue on their end.** What do you do? A strong answer says this is a reasonable, common B2B situation — agreeing to a short, specific extension (with a firm new date) is usually the right call and doesn't require jumping to stage 3, as long as they've actually engaged and committed to a real date rather than gone vague or silent again.

### BUSINESS IMPLEMENTATION

2. **[ME]** Write your own real threshold for when a genuine extension request (like the exercise scenario) is reasonable to grant vs. when it's a pattern you should no longer extend trust to.
3. **[PROFESSIONAL]** Know, before you need it, the very rough shape of what a formal demand/small-claims process looks like for a B2B debt of your typical order size in South Africa — this is a "know where the door is" step today, not a "walk through it" step.
