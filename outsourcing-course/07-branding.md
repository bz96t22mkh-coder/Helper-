### DAY 34 — Positioning: Why a Buyer Should Choose You Over the Alternatives

**Module 7: Branding — Lesson 1**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Write down, in one paragraph and three bullet points, exactly why a security-company or cleaning-company owner should buy from you instead of the alternatives they already have — and get comfortable using that answer every time you talk to a prospect from here on.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. Positioning is not a logo or a slogan.** It's the specific, believable reason a specific buyer picks you over their realistic alternatives. Get this wrong and no amount of good design fixes it — get it right and you barely need design at all to win the first few deals.

**2. Who you're actually competing with in this niche.** Not "every uniform company in the country" — the buyer in front of you is realistically choosing between:
- **A local screen-printer/embroiderer.** Treats a 40-guard uniform order the same way they'd treat 40 birthday-party t-shirts: same process, no understanding of shift patterns, staff turnover, or repeat sizing needs. Usually cheap, usually slow to respond, no real account relationship.
- **A generic online workwear wholesaler.** Cheaper at volume, but no local relationship, no consultative quoting, no one who picks up the phone when a size is wrong.
- **Whoever they already use.** Most SME owners aren't dissatisfied enough to go looking for a new supplier — they've just never been given a concrete reason to switch. You're not competing against "no supplier," you're competing against inertia.
- **Larger corporate uniform companies.** Serve big accounts well, but are usually too slow, too expensive, and too impersonal for a 20-80 person security or cleaning company that just wants someone to answer WhatsApp.

**3. What a specialist offers that a generalist structurally can't:**
- You understand that guard and cleaner turnover (established back in Module 1 — roughly 30-50%/year) means the real need is a *steady trickle of individual replacement orders*, not one big batch every few years. Your process (Module 6 pricing, Module 4 suppliers) is already built around that; a generalist printer isn't.
- Your deposit-then-balance structure isn't just protection for you — it's lower risk for the buyer too, compared to a supplier who demands full payment upfront or one who's informal enough that there's no clear agreement at all if something goes wrong.
- You personally inspect every order before delivery. Say this explicitly — most generalists don't make this promise, and buyers who've been burned by a wrong-size batch notice when someone says it out loud.
- You're reachable directly, fast, on WhatsApp — not a call centre, not a sales rep who disappears after the first order.
- You already have a catalogue and pricing built for exactly their industry — they don't have to explain their business to you from scratch, the way they would to a generalist printer.

**4. A simple positioning template** to force yourself into specifics instead of vague claims:

> "For [target buyer], who [specific pain/need], [your business name] is the [category] that [key differentiator] — unlike [main realistic alternative], we [concrete reason to believe it]."

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

The trap beginners fall into here is trying to sound like they serve everyone, because narrowing feels like turning away business. In practice the opposite happens: a security-company ops manager who hears "we do all kinds of branded clothing for all kinds of businesses" hears exactly what every generalist printer already says. The same ops manager who hears "we specifically supply security and cleaning companies, and we understand your staff turnover means you're always replacing 2-3 uniforms a month, not just ordering once" hears someone who has already solved their actual problem. Specialization isn't a limitation you're apologizing for — it's the entire pitch. Vague claims like "high quality" and "great service" are worthless because every competitor claims the same thing with zero evidence; a specific, checkable claim (personal inspection before delivery, deposit-based terms, catalogue built for this industry) is what actually moves a skeptical buyer.

### EXERCISES

Work these for real — this isn't a hypothetical business.

1. **Identify 3 realistic competitors** in your own city/region: at least one local screen-printer/embroiderer and one generic online supplier. Use the same research method as your Day 1 prospect list (Google Maps, local directories, a quick look at what they show online) — real businesses, not made-up ones. Note what each one seems to offer and what they clearly don't (niche focus, personal service, deposit terms, turnaround promises).
2. **Write your positioning statement** using the template above, filled in with your real business name candidate and real specifics from your own catalogue/pricing/process.
3. **Write 3 USP bullet points**, one sentence each, in plain language a busy ops manager would actually read — no corporate jargon ("synergy," "best-in-class," "solutions").

A strong answer here is specific and checkable, not just positive-sounding. "We personally inspect every order before it leaves for delivery" is a strong USP because it's a concrete promise you can be held to. "We provide excellent quality uniforms" is a weak one because every competitor says the same thing and nothing about it can be verified. If your three bullets could be copy-pasted onto a competitor's site without anyone noticing, rewrite them until they couldn't be.

### DAY 35 — Brand Identity: Name, Logo Brief, and Visual Direction

**Module 7: Branding — Lesson 2**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Lock in your final business name, and write a logo brief and basic colour/style direction precise enough that a designer (or an AI image tool, or Claude Code generating a simple text-based mark) could act on it without guessing what you mean.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. You are not designing the logo yourself today.** Very few beginners have graphic design skill, and trying to design a logo from scratch usually produces something amateurish that undermines the credibility you're trying to build. Today's job is narrower and more valuable: define the *direction* clearly enough that whoever executes it (a freelance designer later, an AI design tool, or a simple text/wordmark treatment for now) can't get it wrong.

**2. How to finalize a business name from a shortlist.** Take your Module 1 candidates and filter them against criteria that actually matter for this business:
- **Say it over the phone test** — can you say it once, clearly, to someone on a noisy call, and have them get it right? Uniform sales in this niche happen over WhatsApp and phone calls constantly; a name that needs spelling out every time is friction on every single contact.
- **Credibility test** — does it sound like a real B2B supplier to an ops manager, not a hobby or a consumer brand?
- **Availability test** — is a reasonable domain variant available (you'll confirm this properly in Module 8, but do a quick check now so you're not attached to a name you can't use online), and is it not already the name of an existing uniform/clothing business in your area (check Google)?
- **Room-to-grow test** — does it still make sense if you expand past security/cleaning into a third niche later (Module 21), or is it locked to one industry in a way that will feel wrong in a year?

**3. What a logo brief actually needs to contain**, at minimum:
- The finalized business name and exact wording (including whether "Pty Ltd," an ampersand, etc. is part of the mark).
- The one-sentence positioning statement from yesterday, so whoever designs it understands who it's for.
- **Feel**: 3-4 adjectives, chosen deliberately for the buyer, not for you. For this niche, the right adjectives are usually something like *trustworthy, no-nonsense, professional, durable* — not *fun, playful, luxury*. A security-company ops manager evaluating a uniform supplier is not looking for a trendy brand; they're looking for one they'd trust with a company-wide order.
- **What it needs to communicate at a glance**: this is a serious, established-feeling B2B supplier — not a side hustle, not a fashion brand.
- **Colour direction**: for this industry, muted/solid, confident colours read as more credible than bright or playful ones — think navy, charcoal, deep green, burgundy, or similar, usually with one accent colour. Avoid anything that reads as "consumer retail" (pastels, gradients, novelty fonts).
- **What to avoid explicitly**: clip-art icons, overly literal images (a picture of a shirt), anything that looks hand-drawn or informal, more than 2 colours in the primary mark.
- **Format needs**: must work as a small square (WhatsApp profile photo, favicon later), must work in one colour (for stamps, embroidery digitizing, greyscale printing), must be legible small.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

A logo brief that says "make it look professional" is useless — every brief says that. A logo brief that says "navy and charcoal, one accent colour, a clean wordmark or a simple geometric mark, no clip-art icons, must read clearly as a small square profile photo, needs to feel trustworthy and no-nonsense to a security-company ops manager evaluating suppliers" gives a designer (or an AI tool, or a future employee) almost no room to misinterpret you. The specificity you put into the brief today is what saves you from three rounds of "no, not like that" later — whether you're paying a freelancer or generating options yourself.

### EXERCISES

1. **Score your Module 1 name candidates** against the four tests above (phone, credibility, availability, room-to-grow) — a simple table, pass/fail/maybe per test, per name.
2. **Pick your final name** and write one sentence of reasoning for why it beat the others.
3. **Draft your logo brief** using the structure above, filled in completely — not placeholders.

A strong logo brief is one you could hand to a stranger designer with zero other context and get back something close to what you have in your head. If your brief still requires you to explain things verbally, it's not finished.

### DAY 36 — Professional Documents: Building Your Company Profile

**Module 7: Branding — Lesson 3**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Get a professional one-page company profile built — the document you attach to a WhatsApp message or email the first time a real prospect asks "tell me more about your company," so you're never caught with nothing to send.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. Why a one-pager matters more than a full website at this stage.** A corporate buyer evaluating a new supplier almost always asks for "something in writing" before they'll take you seriously — and a one-page PDF profile is faster to produce, faster for them to read, and easier to forward internally (to a finance manager, an owner, a procurement contact) than a website link. Your website (Module 8) matters for being found; your one-pager matters for closing the specific conversation you're already in.

**2. What a corporate one-pager for this niche actually needs to contain:**
- Business name and logo placement (even a simple text wordmark for now — you can swap in the finished logo later).
- One or two lines of positioning (from Day 34) — who you serve and why you're different.
- What you supply, in plain terms tied to the niche (branded uniforms and workwear for security and cleaning companies — polos, golfers, overalls, and related workwear, with embroidered or printed branding).
- How you work, in 4-5 short steps (quote → 50% deposit → order placed with supplier → quality inspection → delivery → balance due) — this doubles as reassurance about your process being organized and low-risk.
- Contact details (phone/WhatsApp, email, and once it exists, your website).
- A short line about terms (deposit required, standard turnaround expectations) — sets expectations before the first real quote, so nothing feels sprung on the buyer later.

**3. Why this is a Claude Code build, not a design-app task.** You don't need Canva skills or a paid template — you need clean, correctly-formatted content laid out simply and consistently, which is exactly the kind of output Claude Code produces well from a clear brief. You provide the words and the direction (from your positioning and logo brief); Claude Code produces a clean, ready-to-send document.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

A one-pager that lists everything you *could* do reads as unfocused and undermines the specialist positioning you spent Day 34 building. Keep it to what you actually do for this niche, said clearly, with nothing vague. Corporate buyers skim; a one-pager crowded with paragraphs won't get read past the first few lines. Short sections, real information, no filler adjectives.

### EXERCISES

Before building, draft the actual content in your own words (Claude Code will format it, but the substance has to be yours and accurate):
1. Write your one or two positioning lines for the top of the page.
2. Write your "what we supply" paragraph in plain language.
3. Write your 4-5 step "how we work" list.

A strong draft here reads the way you'd actually talk to an ops manager, not like a corporate brochure — specific, direct, no jargon.

### DAY 37 — Business Email and Professional Written Communication

**Module 7: Branding — Lesson 4**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Set up a dedicated, professional-looking business email address, and define the written tone you'll use consistently in quotes, emails, and formal WhatsApp messages to corporate buyers.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. Why a dedicated business email matters even before you have a domain.** You haven't bought your domain yet (that's Module 8) — but you don't need one to look credible today. A dedicated Gmail address built around your finalized business name (not your personal email, and not something like a nickname or a number-suffixed handle) is a five-minute setup that immediately separates "business" from "personal" in every buyer's inbox. When your domain exists (Module 8), you'll upgrade this to a proper address on your own domain — today's account is the placeholder that gets you moving now instead of waiting.

**2. What professional written communication in this niche actually looks like.** Corporate buyers in South African B2B, especially operations-focused SME owners, respond best to written communication that is:
- **Direct** — say what you're offering, what it costs, and what happens next, without burying it in pleasantries.
- **Specific** — real numbers, real timelines, not "competitive pricing" or "fast turnaround."
- **Correctly formal, not stiff** — professional doesn't mean robotic. Plain, confident, respectful language reads better than corporate jargon.
- **Error-free** — spelling and grammar mistakes in a quote or email are read as a signal about how carefully you'll handle their order. This matters disproportionately for a new, unproven supplier.
- **Action-ending** — every message to a prospect should end with a clear next step ("Let me know if Friday works for a call," not just trailing off).

**3. The difference in tone between WhatsApp and email for this business.** WhatsApp can be slightly more casual (short messages, quick replies) but never sloppy — no textspeak, correct punctuation, complete sentences. Email is where your most formal writing lives: quotes, contracts, anything that might get forwarded internally to someone who's never spoken to you. Match the register to the channel, but never drop below "professional."

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

The biggest tone mistake beginners make in either direction: too casual (undermines trust with a corporate buyer who's putting a company-wide order in your hands) or overcorrected into stiff corporate jargon (reads as insincere and impersonal, which is exactly the weakness you're positioned against generalist/corporate competitors on). The tone that wins in this niche sits in the middle — write the way a competent, established small-business owner actually talks: plain, direct, warm enough to be human, precise enough to be trusted with money.

### EXERCISES

1. **Set up your business Gmail address** using your finalized business name (e.g. a clean, professional variant — no numbers, no personal nicknames).
2. **Write a bad-example vs. good-example pair** for a first-contact email reply to a prospect who asked "what do you offer and how much." Write the bad version first (too casual, or too stiff/jargon-heavy — pick one failure mode and exaggerate it) and then rewrite it well. This exercise is about training your ear for the difference, not producing a real template yet (that's Day 38's brand pack).

A strong "good version" is short, specific, ends with a clear next step, and reads like a real, competent person wrote it — not a template and not a text to a friend.
