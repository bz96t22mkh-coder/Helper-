### DAY 10 — South African Business Structures

**Module 3: Business Setup — Lesson 1**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Understand the real, practical trade-offs between operating as a sole proprietor and registering a Pty Ltd company in South Africa, and decide — with reasoning, not a guess — which one you're starting as.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. Sole proprietor.** This is the default state you're already in the moment you start trading under your own name (or a trading name) without registering a separate company — no registration step is required to begin. You and the business are legally the same entity.

- **Pros:** zero setup cost or paperwork to start trading; simplest possible admin; you can open a business bank account as a sole proprietor at most South African banks without a CIPC registration (bank-specific requirements vary — confirm with your chosen bank).
- **Cons:** no legal separation between you and the business — if the business is ever sued or can't pay a debt, your personal assets are exposed, not just business assets; can look less established to a large corporate customer's procurement department (rarely an issue for your early SME security/cleaning clients, more relevant much later).

**2. Pty Ltd (private company).** A separate legal entity, registered with the Companies and Intellectual Property Commission (CIPC).

- **Pros:** legal separation between you and the business (limited liability — your personal assets are generally protected from business debts and claims, with some exceptions your accountant can explain); looks more established for larger or more formal customers/tenders later; a cleaner structure if you ever bring in a co-owner or investor.
- **Cons:** real registration process and (small but real) cost; ongoing compliance obligations (annual returns to CIPC, more formal accounting expectations); more admin for zero additional revenue in your first months.

**3. The sensible default for where you are right now.** This course recommends starting and operating as a **sole proprietor** until real revenue and real risk justify the switch to a Pty Ltd — typically once you have consistent monthly revenue, a customer or contract size where the liability protection genuinely matters, or a corporate/tender customer that specifically requires a registered company. Registering a Pty Ltd on day one, before you've sold anything, is spending real money and taking on real admin to solve a problem you don't have yet — the same "capital risk before proven demand" pattern flagged in Module 1's business model comparison.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

This is not a permanent decision — it's a "right for now" decision. Nothing about starting as a sole proprietor prevents you from registering a Pty Ltd later once the business has proven itself (Day 11 covers exactly how, so you know the path even though you're not walking it yet). The mistake to avoid is either extreme: registering a company you don't need yet and paying for admin with no revenue to justify it, or staying a sole proprietor indefinitely once real liability risk (large orders, real money moving through the business, employees) has made the personal-asset exposure a genuine problem.

### DAY 11 — CIPC Company Registration Walkthrough

**Module 3: Business Setup — Lesson 2**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Understand, in plain language, what registering a Pty Ltd with CIPC actually involves — so when your Day 10 trigger conditions are eventually met, you're not starting from zero, and so you never treat this course's description of fees or process as the authoritative, current answer.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**[PROFESSIONAL] — everything in this lesson should be verified directly at [cipc.co.za](https://www.cipc.co.za) before you act on it or pay anything. CIPC fees and exact process steps change from time to time; nothing below should be treated as a current, guaranteed figure.**

**The general shape of the process, as it has historically worked:**

1. **Register/log in to CIPC's self-service system** (historically via the CIPC eServices portal or the BizPortal service aimed at simpler company registrations) using your ID and contact details.
2. **Reserve a company name** (optional in some registration routes, since CIPC can also assign a standard number-based name if you skip this) — you'd typically submit your Module 1 shortlisted name(s) in order of preference in case your first choice is taken or rejected.
3. **Complete the company registration application** — director details (ID numbers, contact details), a registered address (can typically be a home address when starting out), and a Memorandum of Incorporation (MOI) — a standard short-form MOI is generally available for simple, single-director companies, meaning you likely won't need a custom legal document drafted for a straightforward setup like this one.
4. **Pay the registration fee.** Historically this has been a relatively small fee (illustratively, in the low hundreds of rand for a simple private company registration) — **do not treat any specific number here as current.** Confirm the live fee on the CIPC website or via CIPC's official channels before paying anything, and be alert to third-party "registration agent" sites that charge a large markup over the official CIPC fee — you can register directly yourself.
5. **Receive your registration certificate and company registration number** once approved — this is your proof the Pty Ltd legally exists.
6. **Tax registration follow-on.** CIPC registration typically triggers automatic registration with SARS for an income tax reference number — but confirm this actually happened and you have your number, rather than assuming it silently completed.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

This entire process is administratively simple in principle — thousands of small South African businesses complete it every month without a lawyer — but "administratively simple" doesn't mean "risk-free to get wrong." Registering under an MOI you don't understand, or with director details or a registered address you didn't intend, creates real complications later. That's why this lesson is tagged [PROFESSIONAL]: not because you're incapable of clicking through the portal yourself, but because a 15-minute check with an accountant or the CIPC helpdesk before you submit is cheap insurance against a mistake that's annoying and sometimes costly to unwind afterward.

### DAY 12 — Business Banking & Bookkeeping Habits

**Module 3: Business Setup — Lesson 3**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Get a business bank account (or a clearly separated personal account used only for business) set up, and start the basic bookkeeping habits that make Module 15's finance tracker painless to build later instead of a retroactive mess.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. Why separate business and personal money, even as a sole proprietor with no legal requirement to.** Three concrete reasons:

- **You can't see if the business is actually profitable** if business and personal spending are mixed in one account — every deposit and expense calculation from Module 1 depends on being able to isolate business transactions cleanly.
- **Disputes and audits become much harder** without a clean paper trail — if a customer disputes a payment, or SARS ever asks questions, "which of these 40 transactions were the business's" is a bad position to be in.
- **It builds the discipline you'll need anyway** once you do register a Pty Ltd, where separation is a legal requirement, not a preference.

**2. Opening a business account as a sole proprietor.** Most major South African banks offer a business/sole-proprietor account option that doesn't require CIPC registration — requirements and fee structures vary by bank and change over time, so compare current offerings directly with 2-3 banks rather than assuming last year's fee structure still applies. **[PROFESSIONAL]-adjacent note:** this isn't a legal or tax filing step, just verify actual current account fees/requirements yourself before choosing, since banking products and pricing change.

**3. Basic bookkeeping habits to start immediately, before volume makes it painful.** A simple spreadsheet is genuinely enough at this stage (per the roadmap's tech stack — Module 15 builds a proper finance tracker once volume justifies it):

- One row per transaction: date, description, customer/order reference, money in, money out, running balance.
- Log every deposit, every supplier payment, every balance payment — the same day it happens, not "when I get around to it," because reconstructing two weeks of WhatsApp payment confirmations from memory is exactly how bookkeeping gets abandoned.
- Reconcile weekly: does your spreadsheet total match your actual bank balance? If not, find the gap immediately, not months later when it's untraceable.
- Keep every proof of payment (EFT confirmation, deposit slip) attached to its order — Day 14 covers this properly for invoicing.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

Bookkeeping discipline started on day one of real revenue is nearly free — a few minutes per transaction. Bookkeeping discipline started retroactively after 40 unlogged transactions is expensive: hours of reconstruction, and real risk of missing something that matters (an unpaid balance you forgot to chase, a supplier payment you can't account for). Build the habit before you need it, not after a mess forces you to.

### DAY 13 — Contracts & Terms

**Module 3: Business Setup — Lesson 4**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Get a simple, real terms-and-conditions (T&Cs) document built — covering deposit terms, delivery timelines, and liability — so every quote and order from now on has something written behind it, directly preventing the "no written terms" mistake from Module 1.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. Why a simple written T&Cs document matters more than it seems.** It's not about being legalistic with customers you're trying to build trust with — it's about having a shared, agreed reference point *before* a disagreement happens, when everyone's memory of "what we agreed" is still unreliable and self-serving. A one-page, plain-language T&Cs document, referenced on every quote, does this without needing to feel like a legal contract.

**2. What it needs to cover, at minimum, for this business:**

- **Deposit terms** — 50% deposit required before any order is placed with a supplier, non-refundable once production has started (state this plainly), balance due on delivery.
- **Delivery timelines** — that quoted turnaround times are estimates based on supplier lead times, and can be affected by factors outside your control (supplier delays, public holidays) — with a commitment to communicate proactively if a delay happens.
- **Sizing** — that delivered sizes are based on the customer-confirmed size chart (Module 1, Day 3), and that a signed-off size chart is what governs any sizing dispute.
- **Quality/liability basics** — what happens if there's a genuine defect (you'll remedy it) versus what's excluded (e.g. normal wear and tear after delivery, or issues caused by care instructions not being followed) — kept simple and plain-language, not an attempt at a full legal liability clause.
- **Payment method** — EFT/bank transfer as the standard method (matching the roadmap's tech stack), with proof-of-payment expected before goods are released (Day 14 covers this in detail).

**3. This is a starting template, not a substitute for legal advice on large or unusual deals.** For your typical early-stage orders, a clear, honest, plain-language document like this is genuinely sufficient and is what most small suppliers in this space actually use. **[PROFESSIONAL]** — for a large corporate contract, a tender, or anything with unusual terms a customer wants to impose, have an actual lawyer review it before you sign; this template isn't built for that scenario.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

A T&Cs document only works if customers actually see it — attach or reference it on every quote (Module 6) and every invoice (Day 14), not just once at the start of a relationship buried somewhere no one reads. The goal isn't a document that exists; it's a document that's actually part of how every real order gets confirmed.

### DAY 14 — Invoicing & Payment Protection

**Module 3: Business Setup — Lesson 5**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Build real invoice templates — a deposit invoice and a final/balance invoice — and establish a proof-of-payment practice, so every rand that moves through the business has a paper trail that protects both you and the customer.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. Why two invoice types, not one.** Your money flow (Module 1) has two payment moments — the 50% deposit before you order anything, and the 50% balance on delivery. Each needs its own invoice, issued at the right moment, so both you and the customer have a clear, dated record of exactly what was owed and paid, at each stage:

- **Deposit invoice** — issued once an order is confirmed, before any supplier order is placed. States the full order value, the 50% deposit amount due now, and the outstanding balance due on delivery.
- **Final/balance invoice** — issued at or just before delivery. States the original order value, the deposit already paid (with its date), and the remaining balance now due.

**2. What every invoice needs, at minimum:**
- Your business name/details and the customer's details.
- A unique invoice number (sequential — e.g. INV-2026-001 — makes bookkeeping and any later dispute far easier to track).
- Date issued and payment due date.
- A clear description of the order (item, quantity, unit price).
- The amount due at this stage, and (for the final invoice) a running summary showing deposit paid + balance due = total.
- Your banking details for EFT payment.

**3. EFT proof-of-payment practice.** Since EFT/bank transfer is this business's standard payment method (per the roadmap), the practice that protects you: **never release goods, and ideally never even confirm an order as "in production," based only on a customer saying "I've paid" or sending a proof-of-payment screenshot — confirm the funds have actually reflected in your account first**, especially with a new, unproven customer. Proof-of-payment screenshots can occasionally be delayed, incorrect, or (rarely, but it happens) manipulated — the account statement is the only reliable confirmation. This is a small discipline that costs you nothing with a genuine customer and protects you completely from the rare bad one.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

Invoicing today is a manual template you fill in per order — Module 13 (Order Fulfilment / roadmap tech stack) upgrades this to Claude Code-built auto-generated PDF invoices once you have enough order volume that manual filling becomes a bottleneck. Building the discipline (clear numbering, two invoice moments, proof-of-payment checks) now means the later automation has a clean, understood process to formalize, not a bad habit to fix.

### DAY 15 — Insurance & Compliance Overview

**Module 3: Business Setup — Lesson 6**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Get a working understanding of the three compliance areas that matter for a business like this — public liability insurance, basic data-protection obligations under POPIA, and VAT registration awareness — enough to know what applies to you now, what to plan for later, and exactly when to bring in a professional.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. Public liability insurance.** This is insurance that covers claims if your product or business activity causes harm or damage to someone else or their property — for example, if a garment you supplied (particularly anything marketed as safety/reflective workwear) is later claimed to have failed and contributed to an injury or loss. **[PROFESSIONAL]** — get an actual quote and policy from a registered insurer or broker once you're placing real, ongoing orders; this course can tell you why it matters and roughly what it covers, but the actual policy, its exclusions, and whether you need it immediately versus once you reach a certain order volume, is a conversation with a broker, not a course. As a starting principle: the more your product range moves toward genuine safety-rated PPE (Module 5 covers reflective/safety wear basics), the more this matters early rather than later.

**2. POPIA basics (Protection of Personal Information Act) — handling customer data responsibly.** You'll be collecting and storing real personal and business information: names, contact numbers, addresses, possibly staff sizes for uniform sizing runs. POPIA's core practical principles, in plain language:

- Only collect the customer/staff information you actually need to fulfil the order (e.g. names and sizes for a sizing run) — don't collect more "just in case."
- Store it reasonably securely (don't leave spreadsheets of customer data in unsecured public shares) and don't share it with anyone outside the business without a legitimate reason connected to fulfilling the order.
- Be able to explain to a customer, if asked, what data you hold on them and why.

For a small business at this stage, following these three practical habits covers the great majority of real-world risk. **[PROFESSIONAL]** — if your business later collects data at real scale, handles particularly sensitive data, or a customer's own compliance team asks for a formal data-handling policy, get proper legal guidance rather than relying on this summary.

**3. VAT registration threshold.** In South Africa, VAT (Value-Added Tax) registration becomes **mandatory once your business's taxable turnover exceeds R1 million in any consecutive 12-month period** — this specific threshold has been stable policy for a long time and is safe to treat as a well-known fact, unlike a tax rate or a CIPC fee. Voluntary VAT registration below that threshold is also possible in certain circumstances. **What is not safe to treat as fixed:** the actual VAT rate itself, and the detailed registration process/requirements — those can and do change, so **[PROFESSIONAL]** — check the current rate and process directly at [sars.gov.za](https://www.sars.gov.za) when this becomes relevant to you, and get an accountant's input before registering, since VAT registration has real pricing and cash-flow implications for how you quote customers.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

None of these three things need to be resolved today, or even this month, for most beginners at Day 15 of this course — you likely have no revenue yet. What matters right now is knowing that they exist, roughly when each becomes relevant (public liability once you're placing real ongoing orders, especially in safety-rated products; POPIA from your very first customer's data; VAT only once you're approaching real turnover), and exactly where to go for the current, correct answer when the time comes. Treating "I'll figure it out eventually" as good enough for compliance is a real risk — treating every one of these as an emergency to solve on day one, before you have revenue, is wasted effort in the other direction.

### EXERCISES

**Module 3 review quiz:**

a. Why is sole proprietor the recommended default at this stage, rather than registering a Pty Ltd immediately?
b. Name two concrete triggers that would justify registering a Pty Ltd.
c. Why does this course refuse to state a specific current CIPC registration fee or SARS tax rate as fact?
d. What's the difference between what a deposit invoice states and what a final invoice states?
e. Why should you confirm funds have actually reflected in your account, not just accept a proof-of-payment screenshot?
f. What is the VAT mandatory registration threshold, and why is that specific number treated differently in this course from a tax rate?

**Check your work:**

a. It avoids taking on real cost and admin (registration, ongoing CIPC compliance) before you have revenue or risk that actually justifies it — matching the "no capital risk before proven demand" principle from Module 1.
b. Any two concrete, measurable conditions — e.g. a confirmed corporate contract requiring it, consistent revenue reaching a level where personal-asset liability protection genuinely matters, or bringing in a co-owner.
c. Because these figures change over time and this course cannot guarantee it stays accurate after it's written — stating a stale number as current fact could cost you real money or cause a compliance mistake, so every such figure must be verified at the official source (cipc.co.za, sars.gov.za) when it's actually needed.
d. A deposit invoice states the order total and the 50% deposit due now, before any supplier order is placed. A final invoice states the order total, the deposit already paid, and the remaining balance due now, with a running summary that the two add up to the full order value.
e. Because a screenshot can be delayed, incorrect, or (rarely) manipulated — the bank account statement is the only reliable confirmation that funds have genuinely arrived, and releasing goods before confirming this reintroduces the cash-flow risk the deposit rule exists to prevent.
f. R1 million in taxable turnover over any consecutive 12-month period. It's treated as a stable fact (rather than needing live verification like a tax rate) because it's a long-standing, well-known policy threshold — but the actual VAT rate and registration process should still always be checked current at sars.gov.za when it becomes relevant.
