## 2. Recommended business model

**Model: branded-workwear reseller/outsourcer, no factory, low inventory.**

You are the middle layer between two people who don't want to deal with each other directly:

```
CUSTOMER (needs 40 branded polo shirts for their cleaning company)
   ↓ pays YOU a deposit
YOU (take the order, choose the garment, manage branding, control quality, own the relationship)
   ↓ pays a SUPPLIER (blank-garment wholesaler + a decorator/embroiderer)
SUPPLIER (produces the physical goods)
   ↓ delivers to you or direct to customer
YOU inspect → deliver → collect balance → customer reorders in 6-12 months
```

You never need a factory, embroidery machine, or warehouse to start. You need: relationships with 1-2 reliable blank-garment wholesalers and 1-2 decorators (embroidery/print), a pricing system, a way to find customers, and a way to look professional and trustworthy before you have a track record. That's what this course builds, in order.

**Why this model over alternatives:**
- *Buying your own embroidery machine on day 1* — too much capital and skill risk before you've sold anything. [NICE TO KNOW for later, NOT for Day 1.]
- *Pure dropshipping with no quality check* — too risky for B2B, where one bad batch of uniforms kills a corporate account permanently. You will always personally inspect before delivery in the early stage.
- *Retail/walk-in shop* — wrong customer (you're selling to businesses, B2B, not consumers) and adds rent you don't need yet.

## 3. Recommended starting niche

You said don't assume every industry — so here's the actual comparison, using the factors you listed, for the South African market:

| Niche | Demand | Profitability | Competition | Ease of acquiring customers | Avg order size | Repeat orders | CLV | Scalability |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Security companies** | Very high (thousands of PSIRA-registered firms) | Good | Medium (fragmented, many SMEs underserved) | High — findable, owner/ops manager is the decision-maker, reachable by phone/WhatsApp | Large (20-200+ guards) | Very high — guard turnover is ~30-50%/yr plus wear | High | High — near-identical spec across companies |
| **Cleaning companies** | High | Good | Medium | High — same profile as security | Medium-Large | High — same turnover-driven logic | High | High |
| Restaurants/hospitality | High | Medium | High (many local screen-printers already compete here) | Medium — many outlets but price-shopping is the norm, high business failure rate | Small-Medium | Medium | Medium | Medium |
| Hotels | Medium | Medium-High | Medium | Low — longer sales cycle, need to get past procurement, high trust bar for a beginner | Large | Medium | High (once in) | Medium |
| Construction | High | Medium-High | Medium | Medium — SANS-compliant PPE (reflective vests, hard hats) adds compliance complexity you don't need on day 1 | Large | Medium | Medium-High | Medium |
| Medical/scrubs | Medium | Medium | High (established specialist suppliers already own this) | Medium | Medium | Medium-High | Medium | Medium |
| Schools | Medium | Low-Medium | High (entrenched incumbents, tender processes, seasonal cash flow) | Low | Large | High but slow | Medium | Low for a beginner |
| Corporate/office | Medium | Medium | Medium | Medium | Small (one-off events, not recurring uniform need) | Low | Low-Medium | Low as a starting point |
| Retail | Medium | Medium | High | Medium | Small-Medium | Medium | Medium | Medium |

**Recommendation: start with security companies as your primary niche, cleaning companies as your secondary/parallel niche.** They are practically the same business:
- Same buyer persona (owner or operations manager of an SME service company)
- Same simple garment specs (workwear shirts/golfers/overalls, not fashion — easy to produce reliably)
- Same repeat-order driver: high staff turnover means you get reorders every few months without doing anything extra, just by staying in touch
- Same sourcing: one blank-garment wholesaler and one embroiderer can serve both niches
- Large, fragmented markets with thousands of reachable SMEs, so there's no shortage of prospects for a total beginner to approach

**Do not start with schools, hotels, or construction PPE.** Too much sales-cycle friction, compliance overhead, or entrenched competition for someone with zero track record. Revisit them in Module 21 (Scaling) once you have cash flow, references, and capital behind you.

Restaurants/hospitality is a good **third niche to add in month 3-4**, once your sourcing and fulfilment process is proven — different SKUs (aprons, chef wear) but same sales motion.

## 4. How the business makes money

- **Gross profit per order** = what the customer pays you − what you pay the supplier (blanks + branding) for that order.
- Typical structure you'll build in Module 6: 40-100%+ markup over your landed cost, a **50% deposit before you order anything** (this is what protects your cash flow — you are never funding a stranger's order out of your own pocket), balance on delivery, and a minimum order quantity (MOQ) so small orders don't waste your time at a loss.
- Once a customer becomes a repeat account (e.g. 30-day payment terms), that trust is earned — not given from day 1.

## 7. Biggest risks

1. **Cash flow risk** — paying a supplier before the customer has paid you. Solved by: always collect a deposit before you place any supplier order. Non-negotiable, taught Day 1 of Module 3.
2. **Quality control risk** — a bad batch (wrong size run, sloppy embroidery) reaching a customer damages a relationship you can't easily rebuild. Solved by: you personally inspect before delivery, every time, early on.
3. **Supplier reliability** — one supplier disappearing or missing deadlines strands you. Solved by: always qualify a backup supplier (Module 4).
4. **Customer concentration** — one big account being your only revenue. Solved by: pipeline discipline (Module 10-11).
5. **Non-payment / late payment** — solved by: deposits, written terms, and a follow-up system (Module 3 + 14).
6. **Underpricing to win the first deal** — kills your margin and sets a bad precedent with that customer forever. Solved by: pricing tool built in Module 6, used from quote #1.

## 8. Biggest beginner mistakes (so you can skip them)

- No deposit collected → you personally finance the customer's order and carry all the risk.
- No written quote/terms → disputes over price, sizes, or delivery dates with nothing to point to.
- Guessing sizes instead of using a size chart → wrong-size garments, redo costs eaten by you.
- Overpromising delivery dates before confirming supplier lead times.
- Trying to serve every industry from day 1 instead of one or two niches you can dominate.
- No follow-up system → leads go cold, one-time customers never reorder.
- Confusing markup and margin (Module 6 makes sure you never do this).

## 10. The Claude Code Business Builder Workflow (reused for every system we build)

1. **Define the requirement in plain English** — what does this need to do, for whom, and what does "working" look like?
2. **I break it into the smallest useful first version** — not the full vision, the smallest thing you can actually test today.
3. **I build it, explain what I built in plain language**, and give you the exact test to run.
4. **You test it** (add a real lead, generate a real quote, etc.) and tell me what worked / didn't.
5. **I fix, or we scope the next increment.**

## 11. What gets automated vs. stays manual vs. gets delegated

- **[AUTOMATION] now-ish:** lead intake forms, quote calculation, reorder reminders, invoice generation, follow-up reminders, reporting.
- **[ME] always:** the actual sales conversation and closing, supplier negotiation, final quality inspection, judgment calls on difficult customers.
- **[EMPLOYEE] later:** admin/lead research (VA), a salesperson once the process is proven and repeatable, an ops/fulfilment coordinator, a bookkeeper.
- **[PROFESSIONAL] always:** tax filing, legal contract review for large corporate accounts, formal company registration paperwork specifics.

## 12. Realistic path

- **Zero → first customer:** ~4-8 weeks of consistent 2-hour days — niche validated, supplier relationships tested with samples, pricing tool built, a real outreach list of 50+ prospects worked, 3-5 discovery conversations, 1 closed deal.
- **First customer → consistent revenue:** ~2-3 months — repeatable outreach + a working CRM + referral loop, targeting 5-10 active accounts and a reorder cadence, so revenue doesn't depend on constant new-customer hunting.
- **Consistent revenue → scalable company:** ongoing — automation absorbing the repetitive work, first hire, expansion into a second niche/city, then eventually export/international.
