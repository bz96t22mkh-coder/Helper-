### DAY 46 — B2B Uniform Sales Fundamentals: The Buyer's Journey

**Module 9: Sales — Lesson 1**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Understand exactly how a security or cleaning company owner or ops manager moves from "never heard of you" to "signed and paid a deposit," so every script and technique in this module has a clear place in a real process instead of feeling like disconnected tactics.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. Who you're actually selling to.** In this niche, the buyer is almost always the owner (in a smaller company) or an operations manager (in a larger one) — someone who is busy, not a procurement specialist, personally responsible for staff looking presentable and for controlling costs, and who has probably never given their current uniform supplier much thought unless something went wrong. They are not comparison-shopping fifteen suppliers; if approached well, they'll seriously consider one or two.

**2. The buyer's journey, stage by stage:**
- **Unaware** — doesn't know you exist. Most of your prospect list (Module 1/10) starts here.
- **Aware** — has heard from you once (a cold WhatsApp, a call, a referral) and knows you exist, but hasn't engaged.
- **Interested/Engaged** — has replied, asked a question, or agreed to a discovery call (Day 48). This is the first real signal.
- **Evaluating** — has received a quote (Day 49) and is weighing it, often against doing nothing (staying with their current arrangement) rather than against a competing supplier.
- **Deciding** — is close to yes but has an objection or hesitation (Day 50).
- **Customer** — has paid the deposit. This is the actual finish line of this module's process — everything after (Modules 12-14) is fulfilment.
- **Repeat customer** — reorders later, driven by staff turnover, without you needing to resell them from scratch.

**3. Why this journey matters for how you sell.** A cold prospect who's never heard of you is not ready for a hard pitch — they're ready for a short, low-pressure first contact (Day 47). A prospect who's already had a discovery call and knows their needs are understood is ready for a real quote (Day 49), not another round of introductions. Selling too hard too early (pitching before you've listened) is one of the most common reasons a promising lead goes quiet.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

The biggest realistic competitor at almost every stage of this journey isn't a rival supplier — it's inertia. Most prospects already have some uniform arrangement, however informal, and "doing nothing" is always the easiest option for them. Your job through this whole journey isn't to prove you're better than a specific named competitor; it's to make switching (or formalizing an informal arrangement) feel low-risk and clearly worth the effort, using the deposit-first, low-risk positioning you already built in Module 7.

3. **Identify what "success" looks like at each stage** — the specific, observable thing that tells you a prospect has moved from one stage to the next (e.g. "replied to WhatsApp" = aware → interested).

A strong answer to exercise 3 is concrete and observable, not a feeling — "they seemed keener" isn't a stage change; "they agreed to a specific time for a call" is.

### DAY 47 — Cold Outreach Scripts

**Module 9: Sales — Lesson 2**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Write real, ready-to-use first-contact scripts for WhatsApp, phone, and email, and get Claude Code to assemble them into a single script-library document you'll actually pull from during outreach.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. What a good cold first-contact message does.** It's short, states plainly who you are and why you're reaching out to this specific type of company, and asks for a small, low-pressure next step (not "buy from me," but "worth a quick chat?"). It draws on your Module 7 positioning without sounding like a sales pitch being read off a page.

**2. Channel differences that matter in this niche:**
- **WhatsApp** — the primary channel for this market (per the roadmap). Short, personal-sounding, never a wall of text. High reply rate if it doesn't read like spam.
- **Phone** — best used after some warmth already exists (a referral, or a WhatsApp reply that's gone cold) or for prospects where a number is easy to find but WhatsApp isn't confirmed. Needs a natural-sounding opening you could say out loud without it sounding scripted.
- **Email** — lower response rate for a true cold-open in this niche, but useful when you have an email address and no phone/WhatsApp yet, and useful as a formal follow-up after a WhatsApp/phone conversation (attaching your one-pager, Module 7).

**3. A first-contact script structure that works for all three channels**, adapted in tone per channel:
1. Who you are + why them specifically (not a generic mass message — mention their industry).
2. One specific, credible reason to listen (your specialization, Module 7's positioning).
3. A small, easy next step (a short question, or a specific time offer for a call) — never "let me know if you're interested," which is too easy to ignore.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

The failure mode in cold outreach isn't usually rudeness — it's being forgettable. A message that could have been sent to any business in any industry gets ignored because it clearly wasn't really about them. Every script you write today should be specific enough to this niche (security/cleaning, staff uniforms, turnover-driven reorders) that it couldn't be mistaken for a generic sales template — even though it will, in fact, be reused across many prospects with just the company name swapped in.

### EXERCISES

2. **Phone call opener** — the first 15-20 seconds of what you'd actually say, written out.
3. **Cold email** — subject line + short body, written for a prospect with no prior contact.

A strong script sounds like a real, busy person wrote it to another real, busy person — not like a form letter. If you read it aloud and it sounds like an ad, rewrite it.

### DAY 48 — Discovery Call Structure

**Module 9: Sales — Lesson 3**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Build a real discovery-call question set that gets you everything you need to quote accurately and sell effectively — staff count, current supplier situation, and real pain points — before you ever mention price.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. Why discovery comes before quoting.** A quote given before you understand the prospect's actual situation is a guess dressed up as a number — it's either too high (you lose them) or too low (you lose margin, or worse, you underquote and have to walk it back later, which damages trust immediately). A short discovery conversation fixes this and, done well, also builds the relationship that gets you the deal.

**2. The categories of questions a discovery call needs to cover:**
- **Scope** — how many staff need uniforms, what roles (guards on patrol vs. static, cleaning staff on different sites), how often new staff join (this tells you reorder frequency, tying directly to the turnover logic from Module 1).
- **Current situation** — do they currently have a supplier, informal or formal? What's working and not working about it? (Never assume they have nothing — most prospects have *something*, even if informal.)
- **Pain points** — the actual, specific frustration, if any: slow turnaround, inconsistent sizing, poor embroidery quality, unresponsive supplier, no clear pricing. This is what your positioning (Module 7) needs to speak directly to later.
- **Decision process** — who actually approves this purchase (the person you're talking to, or someone above them), and roughly what timeline they're thinking on.
- **Budget signal, indirect** — not "what's your budget" (too blunt, rarely gets an honest answer), but questions that reveal it indirectly, like what they currently pay or roughly what order sizes look like.

**3. How to run the call itself**, structurally: open by restating why you reached out and thanking them for the time, ask your questions conversationally (not read robotically off a list — the list is for your prep, not a script to recite word-for-word), listen more than you talk, and close by telling them exactly what happens next (you'll prepare a quote and follow up by a specific day).

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

A discovery call that turns into an interrogation (rapid-fire questions, no listening, no natural conversation) makes a busy owner regret picking up. A discovery call that never gets past small talk wastes the opportunity and leaves you quoting blind. The skill is asking real questions while sounding genuinely interested in the answers — because you are: every answer here directly improves your quote accuracy and your ability to handle objections later (Day 50).

### EXERCISES

1. **Write your full discovery question list**, organized under the four categories above, phrased the way you'd actually say them out loud (not clinical survey language).
2. **Write your call opener and closer** — the first 30 seconds and the last 30 seconds of the call, word for word.
3. **Roleplay on paper**: write out a hypothetical prospect's answers to your questions (a realistic, invented scenario for practice — not a real prospect's actual answers) and note what a good follow-up question to each answer would be, to practice listening-and-adapting rather than just running down a fixed list.

A strong discovery question list gets specific, useful answers, not yes/no dead ends — "what's been frustrating about your current supplier, if anything?" opens a real conversation; "are you happy with your current supplier?" invites a one-word answer.

### DAY 49 — Quoting and Presenting the Offer

**Module 9: Sales — Lesson 4**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Turn a discovery call's findings into an accurate quote using your pricing calculator, and learn how to present that quote so it reads as a confident, professional offer rather than an apologetic price list.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. From discovery answers to calculator inputs.** Your Module 6 pricing calculator needs real inputs (garment type and quantity, branding method, any bulk/corporate rate thresholds) — Day 48's discovery answers are exactly what feeds it. Staff count and roles tell you garment types and quantities; current-supplier pain points tell you what to emphasize in how you present the number, not the number itself.

**2. How to structure a quote document**, at minimum:
- Company and contact details, dated.
- Line items: garment, quantity, unit price, subtotal — clear enough that nothing is ambiguous.
- Total, deposit amount (50%) and balance amount, stated explicitly, not buried.
- Turnaround time estimate, based on your real supplier lead times (Module 4).
- Validity period (how long this quote stands, since supplier costs can shift) and next steps.

**3. How to present a quote, not just send it.** A quote dropped into a chat with no context reads as transactional and invites a purely price-based reaction. A quote introduced with a short message that references what you learned in discovery ("Based on what you told me about your 45 guards and your current sizing headaches, here's what I'd propose...") reframes the number around the value already established, not just the price in isolation. Where possible, walk a prospect through the quote live (call or voice note) rather than only ever sending a cold PDF — you can address hesitation in real time instead of finding out about it days later, if at all.

**4. Confidence in the price.** Never apologize for your price or undercut it before they've even responded ("I know it's a bit much, but..."). If your pricing calculator (Module 6) reflects real costs and a fair margin, present the number plainly and let any pushback come from them — which Day 50 prepares you to handle.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

The most common beginner mistake at this stage is discounting pre-emptively out of nervousness — offering a lower number before anyone has even objected, because sending a "big" number feels uncomfortable. This directly undoes the pricing discipline built in Module 6 and trains every future prospect to expect a discount just by hesitating. Present the calculator's number as-is; if a real objection comes (Day 50), handle it deliberately, not reflexively.

### EXERCISES

1. **Build one full practice quote** using your real pricing calculator and a realistic (invented, for practice) discovery scenario — a company name, staff count, and garment mix you make up for the exercise.
2. **Write your quote-presentation message** — the short note that introduces the quote, referencing specifics from the (invented) discovery conversation.
3. **Practice saying your total price out loud**, plainly, with no apologetic hedge before or after it — say it into your phone's voice recorder if that helps you hear how it actually sounds.

A strong practice quote is internally consistent (the deposit is exactly 50% of the total, the balance is the rest, the math is correct) and presented with confidence in the accompanying message — no hedging language like "hopefully this works for your budget."

### DAY 50 — Objection Handling

**Module 9: Sales — Lesson 5**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Build real, usable scripted responses to the objections you'll actually hear most often in this niche, so a "no" or a hesitation doesn't end the conversation before you've had a fair chance to address it.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. An objection is information, not rejection.** Almost every objection is really a specific, addressable concern wearing the shape of a "no" — treating it as the final word instead of digging one layer deeper loses deals that were actually winnable.

**2. The three objections you'll hear most in this niche, and how to think about each:**

- **"You're too expensive."** Rarely means your price is objectively unreasonable — usually means the value isn't yet clear relative to the number, or they're anchored on a cheaper (often lower-quality or informal) alternative. Respond by reconnecting the price to specifics from discovery (durability, personal inspection, deposit-based low-risk terms, turnaround reliability) rather than immediately discounting. A scripted direction: *"I hear you — can I ask what you're comparing it to? A lot of the time the gap comes down to [specific value point relevant to what they told you in discovery, e.g. embroidery quality or guaranteed turnaround], which matters a lot once staff are actually wearing it daily."* Only consider a real concession (a small volume discount, adjusted quantities) if it's genuine and doesn't erode your margin below what Module 6 established as your floor — never a reflexive discount just to end the discomfort.

- **"We already have a supplier."** Don't attack the incumbent — it's their existing relationship and criticizing it can read as an insult to their judgment. Instead, position yourself as a low-risk backup or a comparison point, and use discovery to find any real gap. A scripted direction: *"Totally understand — most of the companies I work with already had someone before we spoke. I'm not looking to force a switch; would it be worth a quick comparison quote next time you're ordering, just so you know your options? No pressure either way."*

- **"Not right now."** Often genuinely true (timing, budget cycle, no immediate need) rather than a disguised rejection — the mistake is treating it as a dead end instead of a delayed yes. A scripted direction: *"No problem at all — when would be a better time to check back in? I'll follow up then instead of chasing you in the meantime."* This gets you a concrete follow-up commitment instead of an open-ended "maybe later" that quietly goes cold (this becomes a real follow-up date in your Module 10 lead tracker).

**3. The general shape every good objection response follows:** acknowledge it genuinely (don't argue immediately), ask one clarifying question if useful, respond with a specific point (not a generic reassurance), and always end with a low-pressure next step rather than pushing for an immediate yes.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

Fighting an objection head-on (arguing the customer out of their stated position) rarely works and often hardens it. Acknowledging it, then gently reframing with something specific and true, works because it doesn't put the prospect in a position of having to back down — it gives them new information to reconsider with, on their own terms.

### EXERCISES

1. **Write your own full version** of each of the three scripted responses above, in your natural voice, using real specifics from your actual business (your real USPs, real turnaround times) rather than the generic placeholders shown.
2. **Write one more objection** you think you're likely to hear in your specific region/market, and script a response for it using the acknowledge → clarify → specific point → next step shape.
3. **Practice delivering all four out loud**, ideally to another person playing the skeptical prospect, or recorded and played back to yourself.

A strong response sounds calm and genuinely curious, not defensive — if your written response reads like you're trying to win an argument, rewrite it so it reads like you're trying to understand and help.

### DAY 51 — Closing Techniques and Roleplay Practice

**Module 9: Sales — Lesson 6**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Learn the closing techniques that fit this niche's low-pressure, relationship-based selling style, then practice a full closing conversation by writing both sides yourself and evaluating your own performance against a clear rubric.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. What "closing" actually means here.** Not a high-pressure trick — simply, deliberately asking for the decision instead of letting a good conversation trail off without one. Many deals are lost not because the prospect said no, but because nobody ever clearly asked for a yes.

**2. Closing techniques that fit a deposit-first B2B relationship (not aggressive consumer-sales tactics):**
- **The direct ask** — after addressing questions/objections, simply ask plainly: *"Should we go ahead and get this into production? I just need the 50% deposit to place the order."* Works because it's honest and confident, matching the trustworthy positioning you've built throughout.
- **The summary close** — briefly recap what was agreed (quantities, price, timeline) before asking for the decision, so there's no ambiguity about what they're saying yes to: *"So to confirm — 45 polos, embroidered, R[X] total, deposit of R[Y] to start, delivery in [timeframe]. Shall I go ahead and get the order placed?"*
- **The next-step close**, for a prospect who's clearly interested but not quite ready to commit fully: get a smaller, concrete commitment (a confirmed follow-up call, an agreed date they'll decide by) rather than letting the conversation end with no next action at all — an open "let me think about it" with no set follow-up date is how leads go cold.

**3. Why the deposit itself is part of the close, not a separate step.** In this business, "closing" and "collecting the deposit" are the same event — there's no meaningful "yes" without it, since the deposit is what actually starts production (Module 1). Every close should end either with a deposit collected, or a specific, dated next step — never a vague "great, I'll be in touch."

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

Closing anxiety (avoiding the direct ask because it feels pushy) is extremely common for beginners and it costs real, winnable deals. Asking plainly and confidently, after you've genuinely listened and addressed real concerns, isn't pushy — it's respectful of the prospect's time, because it gives them a clear moment to say yes or tell you what's still missing, instead of leaving things open-ended indefinitely.

### EXERCISES

**Written roleplay practice exercise.** Write out a full, realistic closing conversation, both sides, for an invented (practice-only) scenario: a prospect who had a good discovery call, received a quote, raised one real objection from Day 50, and is now being closed. Write at least 8-10 exchanges back and forth, ending with either a deposit commitment or a specific, dated next step.

**Self-evaluation rubric** — score your own roleplay honestly against each line, 1 (weak) to 3 (strong):

| Criterion | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Objection was acknowledged before being addressed | Argued immediately | Acknowledged briefly then addressed | Acknowledged genuinely, then addressed with a specific point |
| Close was direct | Never actually asked for a decision | Asked indirectly/vaguely | Asked plainly and clearly |
| Ended with a concrete outcome | Ended open-ended, no next step | Vague next step ("I'll follow up sometime") | Deposit committed, or a specific dated next step |
| Tone stayed confident, not apologetic | Hedged or apologized for the price/terms | Mostly steady, one hedge | Consistently confident throughout |
| Used real specifics, not generic reassurance | Generic ("great service," "trust us") | Some specifics | Consistently specific and checkable |

A strong self-evaluation is honest, not generous — if you scored yourself a 3 everywhere on a first attempt, reread your roleplay skeptically; there's almost always at least one hedge or vague moment worth catching.
