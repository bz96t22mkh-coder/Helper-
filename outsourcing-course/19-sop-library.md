### DAY 108 — WHAT MAKES A GOOD SOP, AND WHAT YOU ALREADY HAVE

**Module 19: SOP Library — Lesson 1**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Learn what separates a real SOP (standard operating procedure — a written, repeatable process anyone can follow) from a note that only makes sense to you, then audit your actual business against a complete checklist of every SOP that should exist by now.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. What makes an SOP actually usable.** You've already built one full example — the fulfilment SOP from Module 13, covering quote through reorder. A good SOP shares these traits, and it's worth naming them explicitly now that you're about to audit everything against them:

- **Specific, not general.** "Follow up with the customer" is not a step. "Send the payment-reminder WhatsApp template from `business/finance/templates/` on day 3 and day 7 after an unpaid invoice" is.
- **Step-by-step, in order.** Numbered, sequential, no step that silently assumes the reader already knows something you haven't written down.
- **No ambiguous judgment calls left unresolved.** If a step genuinely requires judgment ("decide if the customer is being reasonable"), the SOP says so explicitly and names who makes that call and how to escalate it — it doesn't pretend the step is mechanical when it isn't.
- **Written for zero context.** The test is always the same one from Day 105: could a stranger with no history in your business follow this and reach the correct outcome, unaided?
- **Has a clear "done" state.** Every SOP ends somewhere checkable, not "and then repeat as needed."
- **Dated and owned.** Processes change as the business grows — an SOP with no date is a process you can't tell is stale.

**2. Why this matters more now than it did in Module 13.** Writing one good SOP is useful on its own. A *library* of them is what makes Module 18's hiring plan actually executable — every task brief you'll ever write for a VA, salesperson, or ops coordinator is really just a pointer into an SOP that already exists. Without the library, every hire starts by re-extracting your process from your head one conversation at a time.

**3. The full audit checklist.** By this point in the course, here's what should exist somewhere in your business, either as a standalone document or as clear process notes inside a module's build:

Not everything on this list needs to be a separate polished document — some of it may already exist as a clear, followable note inside a module's build (a README, a section of a build note from the Claude Code Business Builder Workflow's documentation step). What matters isn't the file count, it's whether each one passes the "stranger with zero context" test. Something that exists only as a half-remembered habit in your head doesn't count, even if you're extremely consistent about doing it.

### DAY 109 — FILLING THE GAPS

**Module 19: SOP Library — Lesson 2**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Write the real, missing, or weak SOPs your Day 108 audit surfaced — enough of them that a genuine gap doesn't sit open when Module 20's dashboard and Module 21's growth start putting more pressure on the parts of the business that aren't yet documented.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. The SOP template.** Use this structure for anything you write today — it's the same shape as the fulfilment SOP from Module 13, generalized:

```
SOP: [Name]
Owner: [who runs this today]
Last updated: [date]
Trigger: [what starts this process — e.g. "a new inbound WhatsApp lead"]

Steps:
1. ...
2. ...
3. ...

Judgment calls (if any): [named explicitly — what they are and who
decides]

Done when: [checkable end state]

If something goes wrong: [what to check first, who to escalate to]
```

Every field matters. "Owner" and "Last updated" look like admin overhead but they're what stops an SOP from quietly going stale as your process improves — six months from now, a dated SOP tells you at a glance whether it's still trustworthy.

**2. Writing from memory vs. writing from doing.** The fastest way to write an accurate SOP for something you already do regularly is to actually do it once, this week, narrating each step as you go — not to try to reconstruct it from memory at a desk. Memory skips steps that have become automatic to you; those are exactly the steps a new person would get stuck on.

**3. Don't over-document judgment calls out of existence.** If a step is genuinely a judgment call, the right move is to name it as one, not to force a rigid rule onto it that will produce bad outcomes the first time reality doesn't match the rule. "Escalate to owner if the customer's complaint involves a wrong logo or damaged goods" is more honest and more useful than a fake flowchart pretending every complaint fits a script.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

You don't need to write every gap perfectly today — you need to close the gaps that actually get used often enough to matter, from the ranking you did on Day 108. A rarely-used process can stay a rough note a while longer without real risk; a daily one (like logging a lead into the CRM, or the weekly bookkeeping routine) can't.

2. **For one SOP you wrote, identify the judgment call inside it (if any) and check that it's named explicitly rather than glossed over.**

### DAY 110 — CLAUDE CODE BUILDS THE SOP LIBRARY

**Module 19: SOP Library — Lesson 3**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Get everything you've documented across the whole business — from Module 3 through today — assembled into one organized, indexed SOP library, so any process is findable in seconds instead of buried across a dozen folders.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. What the library actually needs to do.** Not rewrite every SOP into a new format — that would just create duplicate, drifting copies of documents that already work. It needs to be a single table-of-contents document that links to every real SOP by business function, so "how do we handle a late payment" or "how do we onboard a new supplier" has one obvious place to start looking, regardless of which module originally built it.

**2. What the output should look like.** A short index page, organized by business function (matching the categories from Day 108's audit table: Setup & Legal, Suppliers, Pricing, Website, Sales, Lead Generation, CRM, Automation, Fulfilment, Customer Service, Finance, Marketing, Retention), where each function lists its SOP(s) as a link with a one-line description and a "last updated" date pulled from the SOP itself. Anything from Day 108's audit that's still genuinely missing gets listed too, clearly flagged as a gap rather than silently omitted — an honest library that shows its holes is more useful than one that hides them.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

This index is what makes Module 18's hiring plan real. When you eventually write a task brief for a new VA, salesperson, or coordinator, the brief doesn't need to re-explain the whole process — it can just point at the relevant SOP in this library. That's the entire payoff of doing this work now, before you actually need it under pressure.

### DAY 111 — CAPSTONE: THE COMPLETE SOP LIBRARY

**Module 19: SOP Library — Lesson 4**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Do a final real-world test of the library — could someone with zero context actually run a piece of your business from it — and confirm you've absorbed what makes documentation genuinely useful before moving into the dashboard module.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**The real test of an SOP library isn't whether it looks complete — it's whether it survives contact with someone who didn't write it.** A library that reads well to you, its author, can still fail this test, because you unconsciously fill gaps with knowledge the document never actually states. That's precisely the failure mode Day 105's VA task brief was designed to catch on a single task; today you're checking it at the scale of the whole business.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

A finished SOP library isn't a permanent artifact — it's a living one that needs the same "last updated" discipline forever, especially as Module 21 adds a second niche and a second city, both of which will change real steps in real processes (different product specs, different local suppliers). Treat today's completion as a milestone, not a finish line.

### EXERCISES

1. **Quiz — answer from memory, then check below:**
   - Name three of the five traits of a usable SOP.
   - What's the difference between a step that should be rigidly scripted and one that should explicitly name itself as a judgment call?
   - Why organize the library by business function rather than by module number?

   **Check your work:**
   - Any three of: specific not general, step-by-step in order, no ambiguous judgment calls left unresolved, written for zero context, has a clear done state (owned/dated also acceptable).
   - A rigid step is genuinely mechanical with one correct action every time; a judgment call has real situational variation that a script would handle badly — naming it explicitly, with who decides, is more honest and more useful than forcing a false rule onto it.
   - Business function is how someone actually looks something up in the moment ("how do we handle a complaint") — module numbers are how you learned it, not how you'd ever search for it later.
