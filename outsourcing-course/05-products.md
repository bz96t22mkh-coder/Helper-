### DAY 23 — Uniform & Workwear Product Types

**Module 5: Products — Lesson 1**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Know the specific garment types relevant to security and cleaning companies, and enough basic fabric knowledge to talk about them credibly with both suppliers and customers.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. The core product types for your niche.**

- **Golf shirts (golfers)** — a collared, short-sleeve knit shirt; the most common branded uniform item across both security and cleaning companies, especially for office-based or lower-visibility roles, supervisors, and cleaning staff. Usually embroidered rather than printed (Day 24 explains why).
- **Overalls / boilersuits** — a one-piece work garment, common for cleaning staff and maintenance-adjacent roles, and for security roles involving physical/technical work rather than static guarding.
- **Safety vests / reflective wear** — high-visibility vests or jackets, often with reflective tape strips, worn over other uniform items for roles with road-adjacent or low-light visibility needs (site security, night-shift guards). Note: garments explicitly marketed as meeting a safety/visibility standard carry real compliance and liability weight (Module 3, Day 15's public liability note applies directly here) — don't market a product as "safety-rated" unless you've actually confirmed it meets a real standard through your supplier.
- **Jackets / bomber jackets** — a step up from a golf shirt for colder conditions or a more premium-looking uniform tier, often used as part of a package upsell (Day 26).
- **Caps** — a low-cost, high-visibility branding item, almost always sold as an add-on rather than the core order (Day 26).

**2. Basic fabric knowledge — enough to be credible, not a textile expert.**

- **Poly-cotton vs. cotton.** Poly-cotton (a polyester-cotton blend) is more common in workwear because it's more durable, wrinkle-resistant, and holds colour and shape better through repeated industrial washing — a real factor for staff uniforms washed frequently. Pure cotton feels softer and breathes better but wears out faster under heavy, frequent-wash use and can shrink/fade more with repeated washing. For security and cleaning uniforms (frequent wear, frequent washing), poly-cotton blends are usually the more practical default — but always confirm what your specific supplier's standard garment is actually made of rather than assuming.
- **GSM (grams per square metre) — a basic weight/quality indicator.** GSM measures fabric density/weight — roughly, a higher GSM means a heavier, more durable (and usually slightly more expensive) fabric, while a lower GSM is lighter and cheaper but wears thinner faster. You don't need to memorize exact GSM ranges — you need to know to *ask* your supplier for the GSM of a garment you're sampling, and use it as one comparison point (alongside your own hand-feel test from Module 4, Day 20) when comparing two suppliers' "same" product.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

You're not trying to become a garment expert overnight — you're trying to know enough to ask your suppliers the right questions, set customer expectations accurately, and avoid an embarrassing knowledge gap in a sales conversation. Real fluency here builds naturally over your first several real supplier and customer interactions.

### DAY 24 — Branding Methods

**Module 5: Products — Lesson 2**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Understand the real trade-offs between embroidery, screen printing, and heat transfer/vinyl branding — cost, durability, and appropriate use case for each — so you can recommend the right method per product and customer, not just default to whatever your decorator happens to prefer.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

| Method | How it works | Durability | Relative cost | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Embroidery** | Logo is stitched directly into the fabric with thread | Very high — holds up to years of industrial washing without significant degradation | Higher per-unit cost than print, and a setup cost (digitizing the logo into a stitch file) the first time a new logo is used | Golf shirts, jackets, caps — the default, most professional-looking choice for durable workwear that gets washed often |
| **Screen printing** | Ink is pushed through a stencil/mesh screen directly onto the fabric | Good, but can crack or fade over many industrial washes, especially with cheaper ink or heavy detail | Cost-effective at higher volumes (the screen setup cost is spread over more units), but each additional colour in the logo adds its own setup cost | Simple, bold, few-colour logos, especially on larger flat areas (overalls, t-shirts), and higher-volume orders where per-unit cost matters most |
| **Heat transfer / vinyl** | A pre-cut or pre-printed design is heat-pressed onto the fabric | Lower durability than embroidery or screen print — can peel or crack over time, especially with harsh washing or drying | Lowest setup cost, most practical for small quantities or highly detailed/multi-colour/photographic logos that would be impractical to embroider or screen print | Small one-off orders, complex/detailed logos, or a quick sample/prototype before committing to embroidery or print for a full order |

**A practical decision guide, given your niche:** for security and cleaning company uniforms — frequently worn, frequently industrially washed, and expected to still look sharp after months of use — **embroidery is usually the right default recommendation for golf shirts and jackets**, and **screen printing is usually the right default for overalls or larger-volume simple logos**, with **heat transfer reserved for small runs or unusually complex logos** where the other two methods aren't practical or cost-effective.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

You don't have to lock in one single method for your whole business — different products and different order sizes genuinely call for different methods, and being able to recommend the right one (rather than always pushing whatever's easiest for you) is part of what makes you look like a knowledgeable supplier instead of a reseller guessing. This flexibility does mean you may need relationships with more than one type of decorator, or one decorator who offers multiple methods — worth confirming directly with your Module 4 suppliers.

### DAY 25 — Building the Product Catalogue Structure

**Module 5: Products — Lesson 3**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Structure your product range into a clear, tiered package structure (e.g. starter/standard/premium) that's easy for a customer to understand and easy for you to quote from — turning Days 23-24's knowledge into an actual sellable offering.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. Why tiers, rather than an unstructured list of options.** A prospect comparing "golf shirt: R280, overall: R320, jacket: R450, embroidery: +R35, screen print: +R20..." as a flat list of options has to do the work of assembling a sensible package themselves — most won't, and many will just ask you "what do most people get?" A tiered structure answers that question before it's asked, and makes your business look organized and experienced rather than improvised.

**2. A workable tier structure for this niche** (adapt the specifics to what your real supplier/product research supports):

- **Starter tier** — the essential, budget-conscious option: a single core garment (e.g. golf shirt or overall) with basic embroidered or screen-printed branding, no extras. Positioned for a smaller company or a first trial order.
- **Standard tier** — the expected default for most customers: the core garment plus a small addition (e.g. a second garment type, or slightly upgraded branding placement — front and back logo instead of just front), positioned as your recommended, most-chosen option.
- **Premium tier** — the complete package: multiple garment types (e.g. golf shirt + jacket + cap), higher-durability branding method (embroidery across the board), and presentation extras (Day 26 covers these) — positioned for a company that wants their staff to look fully professional and consistent, or as an upsell for an existing customer expanding their order.

**3. Tiers are a starting structure, priced properly in Module 6.** Today you're deciding *what's in each tier*, not what each tier costs — Module 6 builds the actual pricing on top of this structure, using real supplier costs and the markup/margin discipline from Module 1. Don't attach prices to your catalogue yet; get the product structure right first.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

The tier structure should be genuinely grounded in what your real research supports — don't build a premium tier around a product or branding method your actual suppliers can't reliably deliver, and don't build a starter tier so bare it undersells the quality bar you established in Module 4's sample vetting. The tiers exist to make buying easier for the customer, not to create an artificial "upsell trap" that damages trust.

### DAY 26 — Packaging, Presentation & Upsells

**Module 5: Products — Lesson 4**

### TODAY'S OBJECTIVE

Add the packaging, presentation, and upsell/cross-sell details that make delivered orders look professional and create natural additional revenue — without turning every quote into a pushy upsell attempt.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO LEARN

**1. Packaging & presentation.** How an order is physically presented at delivery genuinely affects how professional your business feels to a customer, at very low cost:

- **Individually folded/bagged garments**, rather than a loose pile — a small, low-cost touch (simple poly bags) that signals care.
- **Size labelling clearly visible** on each bag/item, especially for larger orders being distributed to multiple staff members — saves the customer's own admin time sorting sizes, which is a real, felt convenience for them.
- **A simple packing list/delivery note** matching the invoice (Module 3, Day 14) — quantity and sizes delivered, so the customer can check off receipt against what was ordered without confusion.

**2. Upsells and cross-sells relevant to this niche.**

- **Name tags** — a low-cost add-on (embroidered or printed name/role badges) that's genuinely useful for customer-facing security/cleaning staff and an easy, natural upsell on top of a core garment order.
- **Belts** — relevant for overalls/workwear trousers, a small, low-cost add-on.
- **Caps** — as noted Day 23, almost always sold as an add-on to a garment order rather than a standalone product, and a highly visible branding item for the cost.
- **Spares/buffer stock** — not a "product" exactly, but worth mentioning to every customer: given the staff-turnover pattern in this niche (Module 1's roadmap analysis), suggesting a customer order a small number of spare/unsized-yet units alongside their main order is a genuinely useful cross-sell that also smooths out the "we need 2 replacement shirts urgently" MOQ problem from Module 2.

**3. How to offer upsells without being pushy.** The test: is this upsell something that genuinely benefits the customer, framed honestly as an option, or is it a tactic to inflate the invoice? Framing that works: "A lot of companies in your position also add name tags/a few spares — want me to include pricing for that as an option, or keep it to just the core order?" — offered once, clearly, with no pressure, and dropped immediately if they say no.

### WHAT YOU NEED TO UNDERSTAND

Presentation and upsells matter most at the moments a customer is actually forming their opinion of your business — first delivery, and any reorder conversation. Getting these details right costs very little and compounds into the kind of reputation that drives the referrals and repeat orders this business depends on for growth (Module 17 builds on this directly).
