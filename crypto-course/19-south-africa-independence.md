## 3. South Africa: Regulatory Landscape

Before the specific figures, it helps to understand *why* South Africa built this regime the way it did, because the reasoning explains what the licensing status of an exchange actually buys you as a trader. Crypto asset service providers sit at the exact point where the traditional financial system's usual protections — deposit insurance, mandated dispute resolution, anti-money-laundering oversight, capital adequacy requirements — either don't automatically apply or need to be explicitly extended to a new kind of intermediary. Rather than leaving that gap open, the FSCA chose to bring CASPs inside the existing FAIS Act licensing architecture, meaning a licensed platform has actually been vetted against fit-and-proper requirements and is subject to ongoing supervision, rather than simply promising good behavior on its own website.

- **FSCA licensing regime (CASP — Crypto Asset Service Provider):** South Africa's Financial Sector Conduct Authority began licensing crypto asset service providers under the FAIS Act on 1 June 2023. Per a DLA Piper update published 19 January 2026, the FSCA had by then received 512 CASP licence applications, approved 300, declined 14, with 121 voluntarily withdrawn (the balance remained under review). The same update reported the FSCA was actively pursuing unlicensed CASP activity, with 81 investigations initiated and 56 still ongoing. *(Source: DLA Piper, "FSCA Update on Licensing and Supervision of Crypto Asset Service Providers," 19 January 2026 — re-verify current figures, since these numbers move every reporting cycle.)*
- **What this means practically:** using a licensed CASP gives you a regulated, supervised on/off-ramp for ZAR. Using an unlicensed platform is not automatically illegal for you as a user, but it forfeits the consumer protections and AML/CFT oversight the licensing regime is built to provide, and — per the enforcement figures above — is exactly the kind of activity the FSCA is actively investigating, not merely a theoretical gap. Verify a platform's current licensing status before trusting it with meaningful funds.

It's worth sitting with what those application numbers actually tell you. Out of 512 applications, only 300 had been approved as of that report, with 14 declined outright and a further 121 withdrawn voluntarily — meaning a meaningful share of platforms that sought a license either failed to meet the bar or chose to exit the process rather than continue trying to meet it. That spread is informative on its own: it tells you licensing is a genuine filter, not a rubber stamp, and that "this platform applied for a license" is a meaningfully weaker claim than "this platform holds an approved license" — plenty of platforms are somewhere in that pipeline without ever reaching approval. The active-investigations figure matters for a related reason: a license isn't a one-time credential a platform earns and then keeps forever regardless of behavior, it's a standing that's actively supervised, with real enforcement action against operators who skip the process entirely. That's the practical difference the "what this means practically" point above is getting at — a licensed CASP isn't just a platform the regulator once approved, it's one currently inside a supervision loop, which is a meaningfully different risk profile from a platform operating entirely outside that structure (§4 below works through exactly this contrast using Binance as the concrete example).

## 4. South Africa: Relevant Exchanges (Verify Current Status Before Use)

The three platforms below illustrate why "licensed" isn't a single flat status but a scope that varies by platform, and why that scope is worth actually reading rather than assuming.

- **Luno** — FSCA-licensed CASP; ZAR deposit/withdrawal support; license covers crypto advisory and intermediary services.
- **VALR** — South Africa-headquartered; first received FSCA Category I/II CASP licensing in April 2024; in October 2025 was reported by Moneyweb as having become the country's first licensed crypto-derivatives issuer, after the FSCA granted it an Over-The-Counter Derivatives Provider licence plus an additional FSP licence covering CFDs, futures, options, forwards, and swaps on crypto (and other) underlyings. *(Source: Moneyweb, "VALR becomes SA's first licensed crypto derivatives issuer," and Disrupt Africa reporting on the October 2025 FSCA approval; re-verify current scope before using derivatives products.)*
- **Binance** — a global exchange with large liquidity, but it did **not** appear on South Africa's list of FSCA-licensed providers as of the checks above. Treat it as data-only (useful for market data per Module 2) unless you personally re-verify current licensing/legal standing before depositing funds. *(Source: techpoint.africa and techcabal.com reporting on the 2024 licensing round; re-verify current status.)*
- **Rule from Module 2, restated:** use an FSCA-licensed CASP, current status verified at time of use, for actual ZAR deposits/withdrawals and meaningful holdings.

VALR's broader license scope — advisory and intermediary services from 2024, extended by the October 2025 derivatives approval — is a useful illustration of the point that licensing isn't binary. A platform can be licensed for basic advisory and intermediary services without being licensed for derivatives, or vice versa, so the meaningful question is never just "is this platform licensed" but "is this platform licensed *for the specific activity I'm about to use it for*." Binance's absence from the FSCA's licensed-provider list makes the same point from the opposite direction: liquidity, brand recognition, and global scale are not proxies for local regulatory standing, and a platform can be perfectly legitimate and enormous by trading volume while still sitting entirely outside the specific supervisory structure this section describes — which is exactly why it's treated here as data-only rather than as a deposit venue, and why that status needs re-verifying rather than assumed from a course written on a specific date.

## 5. South Africa: Tax Treatment (Not Personalized Advice — Verify With a Professional)

- **Classification:** SARS treats crypto assets as intangible assets. Profit/gain is taxed either as a **capital gain** (Capital Gains Tax) or as **revenue** (taxed in full as ordinary income at your marginal rate), depending on the specific facts of your situation — primarily your intention at acquisition and your holding/trading pattern, not any single rule of thumb. SARS's own position is that all facts must be considered — a long holding period doesn't automatically guarantee capital treatment, and frequent trading doesn't automatically guarantee revenue treatment.

It's worth understanding *why* intention at acquisition carries so much weight in this test, because it's a fundamentally different question from "did you make money" or "how long did you hold it." Tax law in most jurisdictions, South Africa included, has long distinguished between an asset acquired as a store of value or long-term holding (capital in nature) and an asset acquired as trading stock — something bought specifically with the intention of reselling it at a profit as part of a scheme of profit-making (revenue in nature). The reason this distinction exists at all is that it tracks something real about the underlying economic activity: someone running a trading operation is generating income from active, ongoing effort in substantially the same way as someone running any other business, and income tax has always treated business income differently from capital appreciation on a passive holding. This is precisely why SARS insists that *all* facts must be weighed together rather than any single proxy being decisive — holding period is *evidence* of intention (a long, undisturbed hold is more consistent with a capital intention than with a trading intention) but it is not itself the test, which is why a long holding period doesn't automatically guarantee capital treatment and frequent trading doesn't automatically guarantee revenue treatment. Other facts feed into the same picture: frequency and volume of transactions, whether the activity resembles a systematic, businesslike operation, the degree of research and organization behind it, and how the funds involved were sourced and used.

- **Capital gains treatment (2026 tax year context):** the first R40,000 of net capital gains for individuals is excluded; the remainder is included at 40% and taxed at your marginal rate.
- **Revenue treatment:** the full gain is added to ordinary income and taxed at your marginal rate — materially higher in most illustrative comparisons than the capital-gains path.

The size of that gap between the two treatments is exactly what makes the classification question consequential rather than academic. Under capital gains treatment, only 40% of your net gain above the R40,000 annual exclusion is included in taxable income at all — the other 60% is simply untaxed. Under revenue treatment, by contrast, 100% of the gain is added to ordinary income, with no equivalent exclusion. That structural difference is why the same rand amount of profit can produce a materially different tax bill purely depending on which category it falls into, which is also exactly why the classification question is worth getting right — and worth getting a professional opinion on — rather than assuming whichever answer is more convenient.

- **Given this course's explicit trading focus:** if you are actively, frequently trading (which this entire course trains you to do), there is a real and significant chance SARS would view at least some of that activity as revenue rather than capital in nature. Do not assume capital-gains treatment by default — get this properly assessed by a qualified South African tax practitioner based on your actual pattern of activity.

This point deserves to be taken seriously rather than read past, precisely because of what this course has spent eighteen modules training you to do. A trader who runs a systematic framework, executes frequently, actively manages risk and R:R on each position, and treats trading as an ongoing skill-based activity is describing a fact pattern that leans toward the characteristics SARS associates with revenue-generating activity, not toward the passive, infrequent, long-horizon holding pattern that leans toward capital treatment. That doesn't mean every trade or every trader is automatically taxed as revenue — the facts-and-circumstances test still applies individually — but it does mean you should not default to assuming the more favorable capital-gains treatment simply because it would be preferable, and should instead get your actual pattern of activity assessed properly.

- **CARF reporting (Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework):** from 1 March 2026, licensed South African platforms (including VALR and Luno) are required to report customer crypto holdings and transactions directly to SARS — meaning your activity on licensed exchanges is no longer a matter of self-reporting alone.

This shifts the practical stakes of getting your classification and reporting right, independent of the underlying tax law itself: CARF reporting doesn't change what you owe, but it does mean any mismatch between what a licensed platform reports and what you declare is now a mismatch SARS is positioned to actually notice, rather than something that would previously have depended on an audit or a voluntary correction to ever surface.

- **SARS Draft Guide to the Taxation of Crypto Assets:** published 1 July 2026, setting out SARS's draft interpretation of how existing income tax and CGT rules apply to crypto — check for the current, finalized version before relying on any specific interpretation.
- *(Sources: TaxTim, Koinly, and Moonstone Information Refinery reporting on SARS crypto guidance, checked 28 August 2026 — verify current guidance directly via sars.gov.za before filing.)*

## 6. Record-Keeping

Good record-keeping is what makes the capital-versus-revenue determination, and the resulting tax calculation, actually possible to defend — without it, you're reconstructing a tax position from memory months or years later, at exactly the point when accuracy matters most and recall is weakest.

- Keep a complete, dated record of every acquisition (date, amount, ZAR cost basis, platform) and every disposal (date, amount, ZAR proceeds, platform) for both BTC/ETH and any meme-coin activity — your Module 16 journals already capture most of this; add cost-basis tracking specifically for tax purposes if your journal doesn't already.
- Keep records of DEX/self-custody activity too, not just CEX activity — CARF reporting covers licensed platforms, but your own tax obligation covers all disposals, regardless of venue.

The reason DEX and self-custody records deserve their own explicit line, rather than being assumed to follow automatically from the CEX record-keeping habit, is that CARF reporting creates an asymmetry it would be easy to misread as safety: because licensed platforms now report to SARS directly, it's tempting to treat off-platform activity as somehow lower-priority to track carefully, when the opposite is true. Your underlying legal obligation to accurately report every disposal exists regardless of whether a third party also reports it to SARS on your behalf — the CARF requirement adds a second source of visibility for CEX activity, it does not narrow what you're obligated to track and declare yourself. A DEX swap or a self-custody transfer that later needs to be reconstructed from a block explorer, without the context of what it was for or what it cost you at the time, is a genuinely harder record to rebuild accurately than a CEX trade you simply forgot to export — which is exactly why building the habit of logging cost basis at the moment of every transaction, across every venue, is worth the friction now.

## 7. Trading vs. Investing Considerations, and When to Consult a Professional

- The line between "trading" (likely revenue treatment) and "investing" (potentially capital treatment) is a factual, professional tax determination, not something this course — or you — should self-diagnose confidently.

This is worth stating plainly rather than softening, because the skill this course builds — reading charts, running a systematic framework, executing disciplined entries and exits — is precisely the skill that makes someone *good at trading*, not the skill that makes someone *qualified to classify their own tax position*. Those are unrelated competencies, and it's a natural but risky mistake to assume that because you now understand your trading activity in granular technical detail, you're equally equipped to determine how that activity should be characterized for tax purposes. A tax practitioner brings something this course deliberately does not attempt to teach: familiarity with how SARS and the courts have actually applied the facts-and-circumstances test in comparable cases, which is the only reliable way to weigh your specific pattern of activity against the classification question in Section 5.

- **Consult a qualified South African tax practitioner** before your first tax filing that includes meaningful crypto activity, and again whenever your pattern of activity changes materially (e.g., moving from occasional BTC/ETH trades to frequent meme-coin trading).

The reason this needs revisiting on a change in activity pattern, rather than being a once-off box you tick early on, is that the classification test is itself pattern-dependent — a determination that made sense when your activity was occasional BTC/ETH trades doesn't necessarily still hold once you've shifted into frequent, systematic meme-coin trading with a materially different volume and character of activity. Treating the first professional consultation as permanently settling the question risks carrying an outdated classification forward into a period where your actual facts no longer support it.

- **Consult a qualified professional generally** (tax practitioner, and where relevant a financial adviser) for anything beyond this course's educational scope — this course teaches trading skill and process, not personalized financial or tax advice.
