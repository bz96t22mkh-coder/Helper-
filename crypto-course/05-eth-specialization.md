## 1. What You Need to Learn

ETH market structure, ETH-specific liquidity/volatility, ETH's relationship with BTC (including the ETH/BTC ratio), ETH derivatives (funding, OI, liquidations), ETH's ecosystem role (staking, L2s, gas/burn), and ETH on-chain data.

## 2. Why It Matters

ETH is your second primary specialization, and it behaves differently from BTC in ways that matter for trading: it's more directly tied to on-chain economic activity (gas usage, L2 growth, DeFi activity), it has a variable/sometimes-deflationary supply mechanism, and it trades with a meaningful degree of independence from BTC that shows up as the ETH/BTC ratio — a genuinely useful, testable signal.

## 3. Foundational Concepts

One item worth clarifying up front: **ETH market structure itself** (BOS, CHoCH, liquidity, FVGs) doesn't get a separate concept treatment in this module, because structure isn't ETH-specific — the same auction-market logic from Module 4 applies. What *is* ETH-specific, and what the rest of this module actually teaches, is everything that makes ETH's supply, narrative, and relative behavior different from BTC's; you'll get your hands-on ETH structure practice in the chart-marking exercise (§7) and Day 25.

- **ETH's supply is not fixed, and the mechanism behind that is worth understanding in detail because it's the single biggest structural difference from BTC you'll trade around.** Since the Merge (September 2022), Ethereum no longer issues new ETH to miners doing proof-of-work; instead, new ETH is issued to validators who stake ETH to help secure the network via proof-of-stake, and that issuance rate is a function of how much total ETH is staked (more validators staked → slightly higher aggregate issuance, but lower per-validator yield). Layered on top of that issuance is EIP-1559's fee-burn mechanism: every transaction pays a "base fee" that is destroyed — permanently removed from supply — rather than paid to a validator, with only a separate "priority fee" (tip) going to whoever produces the block. The net effect on total ETH supply is therefore a tug-of-war between issuance (which adds ETH) and burn (which removes it), and which side wins depends entirely on how much the network is being used: a period of heavy on-chain activity (a popular NFT mint, a DeFi liquidation cascade, high L2-settlement volume) burns more base fee than the network is issuing, making ETH net-deflationary for that period; a quiet period does the opposite, making it net-inflationary. You can check which regime you're currently in on any public ETH supply tracker (Day 22's research task). This matters practically in a way BTC never requires you to think about: BTC's ~21 million cap and fixed halving schedule mean its supply-side story is announced years in advance and doesn't move with usage, whereas ETH's supply is a live, usage-linked number that a long-term holder needs to track and that occasionally becomes a headline narrative in its own right ("ETH is deflationary") — a narrative you'll learn to treat with appropriate skepticism (Fact vs. Narrative, per Module 1) since a deflationary window doesn't mechanically guarantee a price outcome.
- **ETH as "the settlement layer for an economy"** describes a fundamentally different value proposition than BTC's, and the distinction shapes what kind of news or data you should be watching for each asset. BTC's dominant narrative is a monetary one — a fixed-supply, decentralized store of value whose price case rests largely on scarcity and adoption as "digital gold," independent of whether anyone is actually *using* the Bitcoin network for transactions day-to-day. ETH's narrative case, by contrast, rests substantially on *usage*: decentralized finance (DeFi) protocols that let people lend, borrow, and trade without an intermediary; stablecoins (dollar-pegged tokens, covered in Module 12) that settle enormous transaction volume on Ethereum and its Layer 2s; and a growing amount of real-world asset tokenization — all of which require the Ethereum network to function as *infrastructure*, similar in spirit to a payments rail or a settlement system that other financial activity is built on top of. Because ETH's value case is tied to usage rather than pure scarcity, ETH-specific developments — a major protocol upgrade shipping successfully, a surge in Layer-2 transaction volume, a large stablecoin issuer settling more of its volume on Ethereum — can move ETH's price and narrative independently of whatever BTC is doing, in a way that a comparable BTC-only narrative (e.g., a new corporate BTC treasury purchase) doesn't have an equivalent effect on ETH. This is precisely why you'll track ETH-specific catalysts separately in your market snapshot (Section 10) rather than assuming "crypto moved" always means "BTC's narrative moved and everything followed."
- **The ETH/BTC ratio** is simply ETH's price quoted in BTC instead of in dollars — on TradingView this is the ticker ETHBTC, mechanically just (ETH price in USD) ÷ (BTC price in USD) — but despite that simplicity it's one of the more information-dense numbers in the entire market for a crypto trader coming from single-asset futures trading, because it strips out the dollar-denominated moves BTC and ETH tend to share and isolates *relative* performance between the two. When the ratio is rising, ETH is gaining value faster than BTC (or losing value slower), which in practice tends to coincide with periods where traders are willing to move further out the risk curve — a "risk-on" backdrop that is often, though not always, favorable for altcoins generally (a relationship you'll formalize in Module 9's cycle analysis). When the ratio is falling, ETH is underperforming BTC, which tends to coincide with capital consolidating into BTC specifically — often described as "BTC dominance rising" (introduced in Module 4 and revisited in Section 6 below). The reason this ratio earns a place on your standing watchlist, rather than being a curiosity, is that it's a genuinely *testable*, always-available read of relative risk appetite within crypto — unlike a single asset's price, which conflates "is crypto as a whole going up" with "is this specific asset being preferred," the ratio isolates the second question cleanly, which is exactly the kind of context input your Module 20 playbook will eventually formalize as a market-condition filter.

## 4. Beginner Concepts

- **Staking** is the mechanism by which Ethereum's proof-of-stake consensus is actually secured, and understanding the basic economics of it matters because staking flows are a real, if second-order, supply-side factor. A holder who stakes ETH — either by running their own validator (which currently requires locking 32 ETH) or, far more commonly, through a staking pool or liquid-staking provider (which pools smaller amounts together and issues a receipt token representing the staked position) — is locking that ETH up as economic collateral that can be destroyed ("slashed") if the validator behaves dishonestly, and in exchange earns a yield paid in newly issued ETH (the issuance side of the supply equation from Section 3). Because staked ETH is, by design, less liquid than unstaked ETH (historically it could not be withdrawn at all before certain protocol upgrades, and even now withdrawals queue rather than happen instantly), large-scale shifts into or out of staking are worth watching as a supply-side signal distinct from ordinary spot buying and selling: a large wave of *unstaking* following a protocol change that eases withdrawals, for instance, represents ETH that was previously locked away becoming freely tradeable again, which is a different kind of supply pressure than a whale simply deciding to sell spot holdings. You don't need to become a validator-economics expert for this course, but you do need to recognize staking-queue changes as one of the context inputs Module 8 will have you track alongside exchange netflows.
- **Layer 2 networks** — Arbitrum, Base, Optimism, and others — are separate execution environments that process the large majority of everyday, cost-sensitive crypto activity today (a swap, an NFT mint, a small DeFi position) far more cheaply and quickly than doing the same transaction directly on Ethereum's own base layer ("mainnet") would cost. They achieve this by batching many transactions together off the main chain and then periodically posting a compressed proof or summary of that activity back to Ethereum mainnet, which is what lets them inherit Ethereum's security guarantees without needing their own separate validator set to trust. This produces a genuinely debated tension in ETH's narrative that you should be able to explain rather than just recite: on one hand, L2 growth is a long-term structural tailwind for Ethereum's thesis as "the settlement layer for an economy" (Section 3) — more L2 activity means more real economic usage ultimately anchored to Ethereum's security. On the other hand, because that activity happens *off* mainnet, it generates less of the mainnet gas-fee revenue that historically fed EIP-1559's burn mechanism, meaning aggressive L2 growth can, in the short term, actually reduce the deflationary pressure on ETH even while it strengthens Ethereum's long-term relevance — a real trade-off that ETH bulls and skeptics disagree about, not a settled fact, and one you should hold as Hypothesis/Narrative rather than Fact until you've seen how it plays out.
- **The relationship between gas usage, the burn mechanism, and price action** is a piece of context worth understanding precisely so you don't over-read it. "Gas" is simply the fee unit users pay to have their transaction processed and included in a block; when network usage spikes — a popular NFT mint, a period of intense DeFi trading, general congestion during a volatile market move — gas prices rise because block space is limited and users are effectively bidding for inclusion, and since EIP-1559's base fee is burned rather than paid out, a period of high gas usage burns a larger amount of ETH than a quiet period does (tying directly back to the supply mechanism in Section 3). It's tempting, especially coming from a background where you're used to reading order flow and volume as a fairly direct trading signal, to treat "burn is spiking" as itself a bullish trading signal — resist that temptation. High burn tells you the network is being used heavily *right now*, which is informative background context about network health and activity, but it is not a standalone predictive signal about where price goes next: a burn spike can coincide with either a rally (excited buying pushing up on-chain activity) or a selloff (panic selling and liquidations also generate heavy on-chain activity and gas usage). Treat gas/burn data the way you'll be taught to treat most on-chain data in Module 8: probabilistic context to weigh alongside other evidence, never a signal to trade on in isolation.

## 5. Intermediate Concepts

- **ETH's volatility relative to BTC** is not just a statistical curiosity — it has direct, mechanical consequences for how you size positions and place stops even when you're applying the exact same ICT framework you learned on futures. Historically, ETH has shown larger percentage price swings than BTC in both directions: rallies tend to run further, and drawdowns tend to cut deeper, a pattern broadly consistent with ETH being a "higher beta" asset within crypto — more sensitive to shifts in overall risk appetite, for reasons connected to its smaller market capitalization, its narrative sensitivity (Section 6), and its higher representation among leveraged derivatives traders (below). Practically, this means a stop distance and position size that was well-calibrated for a given setup type on BTC will often be too tight or too large, respectively, for the "equivalent" setup on ETH — an order block or FVG of similar apparent significance on an ETH chart may need a wider invalidation distance simply because ETH's normal candle-to-candle noise is larger, and if you don't widen your stop accordingly you'll find yourself stopped out by ordinary volatility rather than by your thesis actually being wrong. You'll practice this adjustment explicitly in the Section 8 volatility-adjustment drill, but the underlying principle — that the *same* ICT concept requires *different* numerical calibration on a more volatile asset — is one you'll carry forward to altcoins and especially meme coins (Module 14), where this effect becomes far more extreme.
- **Correlation between ETH and BTC, and the windows where it breaks down**, is a nuance that's easy to oversimplify in either direction. Most of the time, on any given day or even week, ETH and BTC move together with a high degree of correlation — both are considered "majors," both are held by the same large pool of crypto-native and institutional participants, and a sharp move in either one tends to be read by the market as informative about crypto risk generally, dragging the other along with it (this is exactly why Module 6 will have you build a "BTC-bias check" into every ETH trade — a conflicting BTC move can invalidate an ETH setup that looked technically clean in isolation). But correlation being *usually high* is different from correlation being *constant* — over multi-week and multi-month horizons, the ETH/BTC ratio (Section 3) trends in one direction or another as capital rotates in its relative preference between the two assets, and during those rotation windows the two assets can genuinely diverge: ETH grinding higher against BTC even during a period when BTC itself is flat or pulling back, or vice versa. These divergence windows are not noise to be filtered out or ignored — they're real, tradeable context, and part of what you're training yourself to do across Modules 5–9 is distinguish "ETH and BTC are just both reacting to the same crypto-wide risk shift today" from "capital is genuinely rotating in preference between these two assets right now," because the second case has different implications for what you'd expect altcoins broadly to do (Module 9).
- **ETH's derivatives market** — funding rates, open interest, and liquidations, the same three mechanics you learned to read on BTC in Module 4 — works identically in structure but frequently differently in *degree*. Funding rate is the periodic payment perpetual-futures traders exchange to keep the perpetual contract's price tethered to spot price; when far more traders are positioned long than short, longs pay shorts, and a persistently high positive funding rate signals a market leaning heavily and expensively long. Open interest is the total notional value of outstanding derivatives positions, and liquidations occur when an overleveraged position's losses consume its margin and the exchange force-closes it. All of that is identical to BTC. What differs is that ETH's derivatives market often runs "hotter" — funding spikes higher, open interest builds more aggressively relative to ETH's market cap, and liquidation cascades can be sharper — particularly during altcoin-favorable regimes (rising ETH/BTC ratio, falling BTC dominance) when speculative appetite concentrates disproportionately in ETH and beyond it into altcoins, rather than staying in BTC. Practically, this means the same "funding is unusually extreme, watch for a squeeze or reversal" read you learned to apply to BTC needs its own ETH-specific baseline — what counts as "extreme" funding on ETH is not necessarily the same number that counts as extreme on BTC — which is exactly what the Section 7 exercise (comparing current ETH funding/OI to BTC's) is designed to get you calibrating.

## 6. Advanced Concepts

- **Reading the ETH/BTC ratio as a regime filter** means treating it not as an isolated signal but as one piece of evidence you cross-reference against a second, independent piece of evidence — BTC dominance (BTC's share of total crypto market cap, introduced in Module 4) — before drawing a conclusion about the broader market regime. When the ETH/BTC ratio is rising *and* BTC dominance is falling, those two numbers are telling a *consistent* story: capital is rotating out of BTC and into ETH (and, typically, into altcoins beyond ETH too), which is corroborating evidence of a genuine risk-on, "alt-season"-adjacent shift worth taking seriously as a piece of market context (Module 9 will have you build a fuller cycle framework around this). But when the ETH/BTC ratio is rising *without* a corresponding fall in BTC dominance — for instance, if ETH is simply outperforming a BTC that itself is also rising, with dominance roughly flat — that's a weaker, more ambiguous signal that deserves more scrutiny before you treat it as regime-confirming; it might just mean ETH specifically had a good week (an ETH-specific catalyst, Section 3) rather than reflecting any broader rotation of capital into risk assets. The general principle here, one you'll see repeated throughout this course's approach to on-chain and derivatives data (Module 8), is that a single metric in isolation is weak evidence, while two or more independent metrics pointing the same direction is meaningfully stronger evidence — never proof, but stronger.
- **ETH-specific on-chain data** — introduced here at a conceptual level and deepened with actual tooling in Module 8 — gives you a window into the underlying economic activity that the settlement-layer narrative (Section 3) is actually built on, rather than relying purely on price action and derivatives positioning. Stablecoin supply growth on Ethereum and its L2s is one such signal: when the total supply of dollar-pegged stablecoins sitting on Ethereum-based rails increases, that typically represents fresh capital entering the ecosystem specifically to be deployed into DeFi, trading, or other on-chain activity — a leading indicator of *capacity* for future activity, distinct from price. Exchange netflows for ETH (the same concept as BTC's, covered in Module 4) — net ETH moving onto exchanges (often read as pressure toward selling) versus off exchanges into self-custody or staking (often read as accumulation intent) — and changes in the staking queue (how much ETH is waiting to be staked versus waiting to withdraw, Section 4) round out this initial picture. Critically, every one of these is *context*, evaluated with the same probabilistic-not-predictive framing you learned for BTC's on-chain data: stablecoin supply rising doesn't guarantee a rally, and exchange inflows rising doesn't guarantee a selloff — they shift the odds and give you a fuller picture to reason with, which is a fundamentally different use of data than treating any single metric as a trade signal on its own.
- **ETH's heightened sensitivity to ecosystem-specific narrative and news**, relative to how BTC responds to its own equivalent news, follows directly from the settlement-layer-versus-store-of-value distinction laid out in Section 3. Because so much of ETH's addressable value proposition is about *usage* — will people build on it, will L2s keep growing, will institutions route real activity through it — news that speaks directly to future usage tends to move ETH more than comparably-sized BTC-specific news moves BTC: a major, successfully-executed protocol upgrade, a significant protocol exploit or hack undermining confidence in DeFi security, and regulatory developments specific to ETH (such as the approval or rejection of ETH-specific investment products and their associated flows) all carry outsized narrative weight for ETH. BTC, by contrast, having a comparatively simpler "fixed supply, decentralized, digital gold" narrative, is somewhat less sensitive to any single piece of ETH-style ecosystem news, because there's less of an ongoing "is the ecosystem succeeding" story for equivalent news to speak to. Practically, this means that when you're building your ETH market snapshot (Section 10) and labeling items Fact/Hypothesis/Narrative, you should expect to find more *live, unresolved narrative* items in the ETH column than in a comparable BTC snapshot — not a flaw in your analysis, but an accurate reflection of how these two assets' stories are actually structured.
