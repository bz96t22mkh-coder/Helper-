# Module 6 — Applying ICT to Crypto

**Est. length:** 4 days (8 hours). One of the most important modules in the course.
**Prerequisite:** Modules 4–5, your ICT Futures course. **Feeds into:** Module 7 (testing), Module 20 (playbook).

**Critical framing:** This module does **not** re-teach ICT. It also does **not** assume your ICT edge transfers to crypto — that assumption gets tested with real data in Module 7. What this module does is give you a precise, honest map of *what's structurally identical*, *what's structurally different*, and *what you specifically need to watch for* before you trust a single crypto ICT setup.

---

## 1. What You Need to Learn

Which ICT concepts transfer directly to BTC/ETH with no modification; which ones behave differently because crypto is a 24/7, no-close, differently-liquid, differently-correlated market; and how to hold your existing strategy as a *hypothesis to test*, not a *known edge to apply*.

## 2. Why It Matters

The single biggest mistake a skilled futures trader makes moving into crypto is assuming "liquidity is liquidity, structure is structure" and trading crypto exactly like NQ/ES. Some of that transfers cleanly. Some of it doesn't, for specific, learnable reasons — and knowing which is which before Module 7's testing phase means your backtest actually tests something coherent, instead of randomly applying futures assumptions to a different market.

## 3. What Transfers Directly (Same Underlying Logic)

These concepts describe universal auction-market behavior — they don't care what's being traded, only that real participants are creating liquidity and reacting to it. On liquid venues (BTC/ETH spot and major perp markets), these transfer with essentially no conceptual modification:

- **Market structure** — the sequence of higher highs and higher lows in an uptrend, lower highs and lower lows in a downtrend, and the shifts between them via Break of Structure (BOS), Change of Character (CHoCH), and Market Structure Shift (MSS) — transfers to BTC and ETH with no conceptual changes required, because none of those concepts depend on anything specific to futures contracts, exchange mechanics, or even traditional finance. They describe how *any* liquid, freely-traded market behaves when real capital is flowing in and out of it: price trends because participants keep pushing in the same direction until they stop, it ranges because buyers and sellers reach a temporary equilibrium, and it reverses when the balance of aggressive buying versus aggressive selling flips — and that logic requires only that real participants with real capital are reacting to price, which is exactly as true of BTC and ETH's deep, liquid markets as it is of NQ or ES. You should expect to mark structure on a BTC or ETH chart using literally the same visual process your ICT course taught you on futures, with the difference (addressed in Module 7) being not *whether* structure exists, but whether trading off it produces the same statistical edge in a market with different participants, different hours, and different volatility.
- **Liquidity concepts** — buy-side and sell-side liquidity resting above old highs and below old lows, equal highs/equal lows marking obvious levels where stops cluster, liquidity sweeps that run those levels before reversing, and "draw on liquidity" as a framework for anticipating where price is likely headed next — remain fully valid on crypto, and arguably become *more* visible rather than less. The underlying mechanism (retail and leveraged participants placing stop-losses and pending orders at obvious technical levels, which sophisticated participants then have an incentive to run before the "real" move) doesn't care whether those participants are trading NQ futures or BTC perpetuals; it only requires that less-sophisticated capital predictably clusters orders at visually obvious places, which happens on every liquid market humans and algorithms trade. What actually makes crypto liquidity *more* mechanically visible than futures, rather than merely equally visible, is that crypto perpetual futures markets publish liquidation-cluster data (covered in depth in Module 8) that shows you, with real precision, where over-leveraged positions will be forcibly closed — effectively giving you a data-backed view of *where* stop-loss-equivalent liquidity is concentrated that traditional futures order-flow tools don't offer nearly as cleanly.
- **Fair value gaps, inverse fair value gaps, order blocks, breaker/mitigation blocks, and balanced price ranges (BPR)** all describe artifacts left behind by displacement — a fast, imbalanced price move that leaves inefficiency (a gap between where willing buyers and willing sellers last transacted) or a footprint of the last opposing order flow before a reversal (an order block). None of these concepts require anything about the underlying asset's identity; they require only that displacement — a genuinely fast, aggressive, one-sided move — occurs, and displacement happens on BTC and ETH for the same reason it happens on NQ or ES: large participants moving size quickly enough that the order book can't absorb it smoothly, leaving a visible gap or footprint in its wake. You will find and mark FVGs, order blocks, and breaker blocks on BTC/ETH charts exactly as you were taught to on futures — the pattern-recognition skill transfers completely; what Module 7's testing addresses is whether *trading reactions to* these patterns holds up statistically in a market with crypto's specific liquidity and participant profile.
- **Displacement and CISD (Change in State of Delivery)** — the aggressive, high-momentum price move that signals a shift from one order flow regime to another, often the first visible sign that institutional-scale participation has entered — transfer directly because they describe *who's moving the market*, not *what* is being moved. On futures, displacement is your signal that large funds or institutional desks are acting with conviction; on BTC and ETH, the equivalent signal comes from large spot buyers/sellers, major derivatives desks, and increasingly automated trading funds moving size — different institutions, same behavioral footprint, because any sufficiently large participant moving quickly relative to available liquidity will displace price and leave the same visual signature regardless of what's being traded. The practical skill of recognizing a genuine displacement move (versus an ordinary volatile candle) transfers one-for-one; what you're building toward in Module 7 is empirical confidence that crypto's displacement moves carry the same predictive value your futures experience taught you to expect.
- **Premium/discount, the Optimal Trade Entry (OTE) zone, and dealing ranges** are pure geometry applied to a defined price range — they describe *where within a range* price currently sits (above or below the range's midpoint, i.e., in premium or discount) and where, statistically, price has tended to react most favorably within that range (the 62–79% retracement zone commonly used as OTE) — and geometry doesn't change because the y-axis is measured in dollars-per-Bitcoin instead of dollars-per-E-mini-contract. Any liquid instrument that trends and retraces can have a dealing range defined on it and a premium/discount split applied to that range, and BTC and ETH both do this cleanly given their deep liquidity. What you should watch for, and what Module 7 exists partly to test, is not whether you *can* draw these zones on crypto charts (you can, trivially) but whether price actually respects them with a similar hit rate to what you experienced on futures — the geometry transfers with certainty; the *statistical reliability* of that geometry as a trading edge is the open question.
- **The higher-timeframe-bias-to-lower-timeframe-execution framework** — establishing directional bias from weekly/daily structure and premium/discount, then dropping down to a lower timeframe to time a precise entry that aligns with that bias — is asset-agnostic almost by definition, because it's a framework about *how to organize your analytical process*, not about any property specific to the instrument being analyzed. It requires only that the market you're trading exhibits structure and liquidity at multiple timeframes simultaneously, which is true of BTC and ETH just as it's true of NQ and ES — a weekly BTC chart has trend and structure exactly as a weekly ES chart does, and a 5-minute BTC chart has its own micro-structure exactly as a 5-minute ES chart does. Where crypto adds a wrinkle you'll need to think through (addressed more fully in §4) is that without a clean, universally-recognized session open the way futures has one, defining *which* higher timeframe candle counts as "the daily" or "the weekly" for bias purposes requires picking a convention (e.g., 00:00 UTC) and staying consistent with it, rather than inheriting an obvious, externally-defined session boundary.
- **SMT (Smart Money Technique, or inter-market divergence)** — comparing two correlated instruments and looking for a moment where one makes a new high or low that the other fails to confirm, taken as a signal of underlying weakness or strength not yet visible in price alone — not only transfers to crypto but arguably becomes *more* useful there than in futures. In futures, your correlated pair (NQ vs. ES, for instance) is a fixed, well-established relationship you inherited from the market's structure. In crypto, BTC and ETH give you an equally clean, and arguably even more reliably-correlated, pairing that is *always available* regardless of what session you're trading in, since both markets trade 24/7 with no session gaps to complicate the comparison — a BTC/ETH SMT divergence, or a divergence between BTC and a broader basket of large-cap altcoins, gives you a genuinely useful and continuously-available second data point to corroborate or question a setup, which you'll build into your crypto-adapted checklist explicitly in §5 below.

## 4. What Behaves Differently — and Why

Not everything ports over cleanly, and the reasons why are specific and learnable rather than vague hand-waving about crypto being "different." Each of the following differences stems from a concrete structural fact about how crypto markets operate — always-on trading, no exchange-defined session structure, a different participant base, or added correlation layers — and each one is something Module 7 will have you verify empirically rather than take on faith.

- **No session close, and therefore no single, universally-recognized daily or weekly "open" the way futures has one.** Futures markets have an explicit institutional event — Globex opens at a defined time, the market has defined settlement, and "the daily open" or "the weekly open" is a moment every participant in that market observes simultaneously. Crypto trades continuously, 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, with no exchange bell and no settlement process to anchor a candle boundary — the "00:00 UTC daily candle" convention that most charting platforms default to is a widely-adopted convention, not an event the market itself observes the way it observes a real session open. Your AMD (Accumulation-Manipulation-Distribution)/Power-of-3 framework still applies perfectly well to whatever candle period you define — the accumulate-manipulate-distribute cycle is a behavioral pattern, not something tied to a specific clock time — but you should hold "the open" itself with less weight than you're used to giving it on futures, since it doesn't carry the same unique institutional significance that a genuine session open, observed simultaneously by every participant in the market, carries.
- **True 24/7 trading means killzones are look-alikes, not identical, to what you learned on futures**, and understanding the difference in *mechanism* matters more than memorizing new hours. Futures killzones (London, New York, Asia) are tied directly to when specific exchanges and the trading desks that operate through them are physically open for business — the killzone exists because real institutional order flow genuinely concentrates during those hours and thins outside them. Crypto has no equivalent exchange-hours constraint, but high-liquidity, high-volatility windows still cluster — just around different anchors: roughly the overlapping hours where major global trading desks (crypto-native and traditional finance alike) are active, which loosely resembles a blended London/New York window, plus a second, distinct category of high-volatility windows built around *scheduled macro catalysts* like CPI releases or FOMC announcements, which increasingly move BTC and ETH the way they move traditional risk assets as more traditional capital has entered crypto. Crucially, you cannot simply assume your memorized futures killzone hours apply unchanged to crypto — the §6 exercise has you empirically identify BTC's actual highest-volume hours from real data, because the *right* answer here is a testable fact about the current market, not a fact you can carry over from a different asset class.
- **Weekend liquidity is thinner, and behaves differently, in ways that cut both directions.** Futures markets simply don't trade on weekends — there's no data to reason about because the market is closed. Crypto keeps trading through the weekend, but with materially reduced participation from institutional desks, market makers, and other large, liquidity-providing participants who are generally off during traditional business off-hours, leaving a market that's technically open but thinner underneath. This thinness can produce two genuinely different outcomes depending on conditions, and you should resist the urge to assume you already know which: sometimes thinner weekend liquidity produces a *cleaner* setup — fewer competing participants means a liquidity sweep into Monday's open can look textbook-clean, uncomplicated by the noise of a fully-participating market. Other times, that same thinness produces the opposite — sharper, more erratic, lower-quality moves, because there isn't enough depth in the order book to absorb even moderate-sized orders smoothly, so price can whipsaw on relatively little volume. Which of these describes weekend BTC/ETH behavior in practice, and whether it's even consistent enough to build a rule around, is explicitly a question for Module 7's testing — it is not something you should assume in either direction.
- **Volatility regime differs by asset in a way that requires asset-specific calibration, not one fixed number carried over from NQ.** On your futures course, you likely developed intuitions for "how far" a normal stop distance or a normal OTE retracement should be, calibrated to NQ or ES's typical range of movement. Those intuitions do not transfer numerically to crypto, because crypto's volatility landscape is a spectrum: BTC's volatility, while still meaningfully higher than most traditional macro assets, is the *lowest* volatility you'll typically encounter within crypto; ETH runs hotter than BTC (Module 5, §5); and once you move beyond the two majors into altcoins and especially meme coins (Module 14), volatility becomes categorically different — routinely large enough that futures-scale stop distances would be almost meaningless. The OTE logic and the general concept of "stop beyond the invalidation point" both still apply at every point on this spectrum, but the actual distance in price or in percentage terms that constitutes a sensible stop needs to be recalibrated for each asset you trade, which is part of why this course dedicates entire specialization modules (5, and later 14) to characterizing each asset's own volatility profile before you trade it.
- **BTC dominance and inter-asset correlation add a systemic context layer that futures trading, where you're generally focused on a single instrument's own structure, doesn't confront in the same way.** In futures, if your NQ read is technically correct, NQ generally does what your read predicted (subject to ordinary market noise) — there isn't a second, larger instrument whose independent moves can override your specific instrument's structure. In crypto, an ETH long can be *technically correct* by every ICT criterion on the ETH chart itself and still fail, not because your ETH-specific read was wrong, but because BTC suddenly sold off and dragged the entire market — including a technically-sound ETH setup — down with it, since BTC's price action functions as a systemic risk factor for nearly everything else in crypto (this is the flip side of the strong BTC/ETH correlation covered in Module 5, §5). This is precisely why "what is BTC doing right now" needs to become a mechanical check you run *before* trusting any altcoin or ETH setup, not an afterthought — a discipline this course formalizes as the BTC-bias check in the checklist below (§5) and, later, as a standing filter in your Module 20 playbook.
- **News and catalyst structure is different, and specifically it's additive rather than substitutive** — futures traders already account for scheduled macro data (CPI, FOMC, NFP) moving markets with well-telegraphed timing; crypto traders need to account for that *same* macro calendar (since BTC and ETH increasingly trade as risk assets sensitive to the same macro releases) *plus* an entirely separate category of crypto-native catalysts that don't exist in traditional markets and often arrive with far less advance warning: a major exchange listing announcement, a protocol upgrade landing (successfully or not), a regulatory headline specific to crypto, or an ETF-flow data release. Because several of these crypto-specific catalysts can appear with little or no scheduled warning — unlike a macro release you can mark on a calendar weeks in advance — they carry a real risk of overriding otherwise-clean technical structure with much less notice than you're used to planning around, which is part of why your crypto-adapted checklist (§5) explicitly adds a catalyst check that has no direct equivalent in a pure futures process.
- **SMT changes shape** in the sense that the *correlated pair* you use for inter-market divergence needs to be deliberately reselected for crypto rather than assumed to carry over from futures' index relationships. On futures, NQ-versus-ES is a well-established pairing built on both indices reflecting the same broad equity market from different angles. In crypto, the cleanest and most consistently available equivalent is BTC-versus-ETH — both large, liquid, and highly (if imperfectly) correlated, giving you a divergence signal (one making a new high or low that the other fails to confirm) that's available around the clock, unlike NQ/ES which shares futures' trading-hours constraints. A second, related option is BTC versus a broader basket of large-cap altcoins, useful when you want a read on "is this altcoin/ETH move broad-based across the market, or isolated." The underlying SMT *logic* — divergence signals hidden weakness or strength — transfers without modification; what you must actively reselect, rather than assume, is *which pair* embodies that logic best in a crypto context.

## 5. Building Your Crypto-Adapted Checklist (Draft — Refined in Module 7)

Before you ever mark a setup as valid on BTC/ETH, check:
1. **HTF bias** — the same mechanical process as futures: establish directional bias from weekly and daily structure, and locate current price within its premium or discount relative to the relevant dealing range (§3). Nothing about this step changes moving into crypto; it's included here purely for completeness, since it remains the foundation every other check sits on top of.
2. **BTC-bias check** (new, crypto-specific) — before trusting a setup on ETH or any altcoin, explicitly check what BTC's own structure and immediate price action are doing. This step has no equivalent in a single-instrument futures process, and skipping it is the single most common way a technically correct altcoin/ETH read gets invalidated by a systemic BTC move you never checked for (§4). In practice this means glancing at BTC's HTF and immediate structure every time, not just when something already feels unstable.
3. **SMT check** (adapted) — look for BTC/ETH divergence, or BTC-versus-alt-basket divergence (§3), and ask whether it corroborates or contradicts the setup you're considering. A divergence that agrees with your directional read adds a small amount of corroborating evidence; a divergence that contradicts it is a reason for caution, not necessarily a reason to abandon the setup outright, but a flag worth weighing.
4. **Liquidity condition** (adapted) — determine whether you're looking at a normal weekday liquidity window or a thinner weekend/off-hours window (§4), and adjust your risk expectations accordingly — a sweep or a reaction that would be reliable in full weekday liquidity carries different (and, per Module 7, not yet empirically confirmed) reliability in thin weekend conditions.
5. **Catalyst check** (new) — check whether a scheduled macro release or a known crypto-specific catalyst (an exchange listing, a token unlock, a protocol upgrade date) is imminent, since either can override clean technical structure with limited warning (§4). This is a habit borrowed partly from futures (macro calendar awareness) and extended to cover crypto-native events futures traders never had to track.
6. **Everything else** — market structure, liquidity concepts, FVG/order block, displacement, premium/discount, OTE: apply these exactly as you learned them on futures (§3), since nothing about them requires crypto-specific adaptation.

This checklist is a **draft hypothesis**, not a proven edge. Module 7 exists to find out whether trading it produces a real statistical edge, and to refine steps 2–5 based on actual results.
